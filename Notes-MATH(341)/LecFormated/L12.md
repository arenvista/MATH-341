# Matrix Decompositions: EVD and SVD

## I. From Eigenvalue Decomposition to Singular Value Decomposition

### 1. Eigenvalue Decomposition (EVD)

The standard eigenvalue decomposition applies to **square** matrices. In general, if $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is diagonalizable, then

$$
A = X \Lambda X^{-1}.
$$

A particularly important special case is when $A$ is **real symmetric**. Then its eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormally, so

$$
A = Q \Lambda Q^T,
$$

where $Q$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda$ is diagonal.

> [!imp] Important refinement
> The factorization $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$ holds for **real symmetric matrices**.
> If $A$ is moreover **symmetric positive definite (SPD)**, then all eigenvalues are positive:
> $$
> \lambda_i > 0.
> $$

### 2. Why SVD Is Needed

EVD is limited:

- it requires $A$ to be square,
- orthogonal diagonalization requires symmetry,
- some square matrices are not diagonalizable.

So what if $A$ is rectangular, or not symmetric, or not diagonalizable?

This motivates the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)**, which applies to **every** matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$.

---

## II. Motivation for SVD

> [!imp] Motivations for SVD
> 1. **Algebraic:** Every matrix is orthogonally equivalent to a diagonal-like matrix.
> 2. **Geometric:** Any linear map sends the unit sphere to a hyperellipse, and SVD identifies its principal axes and axis lengths.

> [!def] Orthogonal Equivalence
> Two matrices $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ are **equivalent** if there exist invertible matrices
> $$
> X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \qquad Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}
> $$
> such that
> $$
> A = X B Y.
> $$
> If $X$ and $Y$ are orthogonal, then $A$ and $B$ are **orthogonally equivalent**.

SVD says that every matrix is orthogonally equivalent to a rectangular diagonal matrix.

---

# III. Singular Value Decomposition: Formal Statement

> [!thm] Singular Value Decomposition
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ be any matrix of rank $r$. Then there exist orthogonal matrices
> $$
> U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \qquad V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}
> $$
> and a rectangular diagonal matrix
> $$
> \Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}
> $$
> such that
> $$
> A = U \Sigma V^T.
> $$
> The diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ are the singular values
> $$
> \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0,
> $$
> and all remaining diagonal entries are zero.

## 1. Components of the SVD

- $U$: orthogonal matrix whose columns $u_1,\dots,u_n$ are the **left singular vectors**
- $V$: orthogonal matrix whose columns $v_1,\dots,v_m$ are the **right singular vectors**
- $\Sigma$: rectangular diagonal matrix containing the singular values

Its structure is

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_1 &        &        &        \\
         & \sigma_2 &      &        \\
         &        & \ddots &        \\
         &        &        & \sigma_r \\
         &        &        &        \\
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
A^T = V \Sigma^T U^T.
$$

---

## 2. Where Singular Values Come From

A key logical connection is that singular values come from eigenvalues of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$.

> [!thm] Singular Values and Eigenvalues
> For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$:
> - $A^T A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite in $\mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$,
> - $A A^T$ is symmetric positive semidefinite in $\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$,
> - the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ are the same,
> - these nonzero eigenvalues are exactly
> $$
> \sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_r^2.
> $$

Thus, if

$$
A^T A v_i = \sigma_i^2 v_i,
$$

then $v_i$ is a right singular vector, and for $\sigma_i > 0$ we define

$$
u_i = \frac{A v_i}{\sigma_i}.
$$

This gives the fundamental relations

$$
A v_i = \sigma_i u_i,
\qquad
A^T u_i = \sigma_i v_i.
$$

These identities explain nearly everything geometric and algebraic about SVD.

# IV. Geometric Interpretation of SVD

## 1. Three-Step View of a Linear Map

The factorization

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
$$

can be interpreted as three successive transformations:

1. **$V^T$**: rotate or reflect in the input space $\mathbb{R}^m$,
2. **$\Sigma$**: stretch or compress along coordinate axes,
3. **$U$**: rotate or reflect into the output space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

So SVD separates a linear map into:

- an orthogonal change of input coordinates,
- pure axis scaling,
- an orthogonal change of output coordinates.

## 2. Image of the Unit Sphere

Let $S$ be the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^m$:

$$
S = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^m : \|x\|_2 = 1\}.
$$

Then the image $A(S)$ is a **hyperellipse** in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

- The directions $v_i$ in the domain are the principal input axes.
- The singular values $\sigma_i$ are the semiaxis lengths.
- The directions $u_i$ give the principal output directions.

More precisely,

$$
A v_i = \sigma_i u_i.
$$

So the unit vector $v_i$ gets mapped to a vector of length $\sigma_i$ in direction $u_i$.

# V. SVD and the Four Fundamental Subspaces

A major strength of SVD is that it gives orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces.

## 1. Action on Singular Vectors

For $i=1,\dots,m$ and $j=1,\dots,n$,

$$
A v_i =
\begin{cases}
\sigma_i u_i, & i=1,\dots,r, \\
0, & i=r+1,\dots,m,
\end{cases}
$$

and

$$
A^T u_i =
\begin{cases}
\sigma_i v_i, & i=1,\dots,r, \\
0, & i=r+1,\dots,n.
\end{cases}
$$

So:

- the first $r$ right singular vectors map to the first $r$ left singular vectors,
- the remaining right singular vectors lie in $\operatorname{Null}(A)$,
- the remaining left singular vectors lie in $\operatorname{Null}(A^T)$.

## 2. Orthonormal Bases for the Fundamental Subspaces

From the relations above, we get:

- **Column space / range of $A$**
  $$
  \operatorname{Range}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}
  $$

- **Null space of $A$**
  $$
  \operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{v_{r+1},\dots,v_m\}
  $$

- **Row space / range of $A^T$**
  $$
  \operatorname{Range}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_r\}
  $$

- **Left null space**
  $$
  \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{u_{r+1},\dots,u_n\}
  $$

> [!imp] Fundamental subspace relations
> $$
> \operatorname{Range}(A) = \operatorname{Null}(A^T)^\perp,
> \qquad
> \operatorname{Range}(A^T) = \operatorname{Null}(A)^\perp.
> $$

SVD makes these orthogonal decompositions completely explicit.

# VI. Full, Reduced, and Truncated SVD

## 1. Full SVD

The full SVD is

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T,
$$

with

- $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$,
- $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$,
- $V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$.

This is conceptually complete, but often larger than needed.

## 2. Reduced (Economy) SVD

If $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$, we may keep only the singular vectors corresponding to nonzero singular values:

$$
A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T,
$$

where

- $U_r \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$,
- $\Sigma_r \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$,
- $V_r \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$.

This is the **reduced** or **economy-size** SVD.

## 3. Truncated Rank-$k$ Approximation

If we keep only the largest $k \le r$ singular values, then

$$
A \approx U_k \Sigma_k V_k^T.
$$

This is a **rank-$k$ approximation** of $A$.

> [!imp] Why truncation matters
> Truncated SVD is the foundation of:
> - PCA,
> - data compression,
> - low-rank approximation,
> - denoising.

In fact, the truncated SVD gives the best rank-$k$ approximation in both the spectral and Frobenius norms.

# VII. Applications of SVD

## 1. Matrix Norms

Because orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean length, the size of $A$ is determined entirely by its singular values.

### a. Spectral Norm / Operator 2-Norm

The matrix 2-norm is

$$
\|A\|_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2}.
$$

> [!thm] Spectral Norm and Largest Singular Value
> $$
> \|A\|_2 = \sigma_1.
> $$

So the largest singular value is exactly the maximum stretching factor of the matrix.

### b. Frobenius Norm

The Frobenius norm is

$$
\|A\|_F
=
\left(\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2\right)^{1/2}
=
\left(\operatorname{trace}(A^T A)\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Using the SVD,

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
A^T A = V \Sigma^T \Sigma V^T.
$$

Now use cyclic invariance of the trace:

$$
\operatorname{trace}(A^T A)
=
\operatorname{trace}(V \Sigma^T \Sigma V^T)
=
\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma^T \Sigma V^T V)
=
\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma^T \Sigma).
$$

Since $\Sigma^T \Sigma$ is diagonal with entries $\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_r^2$,

$$
\operatorname{trace}(A^T A) = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\|A\|_F = \|\Sigma\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \cdots + \sigma_r^2}.
$$

---

## 2. Matrix Condition Number

The condition number measures sensitivity of a linear system to perturbations.

For an invertible square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$,

$$
\kappa_2(A) = \|A\|_2 \, \|A^{-1}\|_2.
$$

If

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T,
$$

then

$$
A^{-1} = V \Sigma^{-1} U^T.
$$

The singular values of $A^{-1}$ are

$$
\frac{1}{\sigma_1}, \frac{1}{\sigma_2}, \dots, \frac{1}{\sigma_n}.
$$

Hence

$$
\|A^{-1}\|_2 = \frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A)} = \frac{1}{\sigma_n},
$$

and therefore

$$
\kappa_2(A)
=
\|A\|_2 \|A^{-1}\|_2
=
\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_n}.
$$

> [!imp] Interpretation
> A large condition number means the system is ill-conditioned:
> small perturbations in data may cause large changes in the solution.

---

## 3. Why QR Is Better Than Normal Equations for Least Squares

To solve least-squares problems, one may form the normal equations

$$
A^T A x = A^T b.
$$

But this is often numerically unstable because the conditioning gets worse:

$$
\kappa_2(A^T A) = \kappa_2(A)^2.
$$

### Why?

The singular values of $A^T A$ are

$$
\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2.
$$

So, if $A$ has full column rank,

$$
\kappa_2(A^T A)
=
\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_r^2}
=
\left(\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_r}\right)^2
=
\kappa_2(A)^2.
$$

> [!imp] Numerical consequence
> Forming $A^T A$ squares the condition number, which amplifies floating-point error.
> This is why **QR** and **SVD** are preferred over normal equations in practice.

# VIII. Summary of the Core Identities

## 1. SVD Factorization

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
$$

## 2. Singular Vector Relations

$$
A v_i = \sigma_i u_i,
\qquad
A^T u_i = \sigma_i v_i
$$

## 3. Singular Values from Eigenvalues

$$
A^T A v_i = \sigma_i^2 v_i,
\qquad
A A^T u_i = \sigma_i^2 u_i
$$

## 4. Fundamental Subspaces

$$
\operatorname{Range}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_r\}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{v_{r+1},\dots,v_m\}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Range}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_r\}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{u_{r+1},\dots,u_n\}
$$

## 5. Norms and Conditioning

$$
\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1
$$

$$
\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2}
$$

For invertible square $A$,

$$
\kappa_2(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}.
$$

---

# IX. Key Takeaway

> [!imp] Main idea
> SVD is the most general and most useful matrix decomposition:
> - it works for **every** matrix,
> - it reveals the geometry of a linear map,
> - it gives orthonormal bases for the fundamental subspaces,
> - it explains matrix norms, conditioning, PCA, compression, and least squares.
