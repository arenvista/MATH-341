# Solving Single Nonlinear Equations

## The Root-Finding Problem

We want to solve a nonlinear equation of the form

$$
f(x)=0.
$$

In general, closed-form formulas may not exist, so we use **numerical methods** to approximate a root.

---

## The Intermediate Value Theorem

> [!thm] Intermediate Value Theorem
> Let $I=[a,b]$ and let $f:I\to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous on $I$. If
> $$
> f(a)<0<f(b)
> \quad \text{or} \quad
> f(a)>0>f(b),
> $$
> then there exists some $c\in(a,b)$ such that
> $$
> f(c)=0.
> $$

### Why This Matters for Root-Finding

The Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees the **existence of a root** whenever a continuous function changes sign on an interval. It does **not** tell us the exact value of the root, but it tells us a root is somewhere in the interval.

This is the theoretical basis for many root-finding methods.

# Newton's Method

## Overview

> [!def] Newton's Method
> Newton's method is an iterative technique for approximating solutions of
> $$
> f(x)=0.
> $$
> Starting from an initial guess $x_0$, it generates a sequence
> $$
> x_1,x_2,\dots
> $$
> intended to converge to a root $x^*$.

The central idea is to replace the nonlinear function by its **tangent line** near the current iterate.

### Geometric Interpretation

At the current point $(x_n,f(x_n))$:

1. Draw the tangent line to the graph of $f$.
2. Find where that tangent line crosses the $x$-axis.
3. Use that $x$-intercept as the next approximation $x_{n+1}$.

If the tangent line is a good local approximation, then $x_{n+1}$ is closer to the true root than $x_n$.

---

## Advantages and Limitations

### Advantages

- Often converges **very rapidly** when the initial guess is close to the root.
- For a simple root, convergence is typically **quadratic**.
- Easy to implement when $f$ and $f'$ are available.

### Limitations

- Requires computing $f'(x)$.
- Can fail if $f'(x_n)=0$ or is very small.
- May diverge if the initial guess is poor.
- Can behave badly near multiple roots or inflection points.

> [!imp] Important
> Newton's method is a **local** method: it works well when started sufficiently close to a root, but it does not always converge from an arbitrary starting point.

---

# Derivation of Newton's Method

## Using Taylor's Theorem

Suppose $f$ is sufficiently smooth. Expanding $f(x)$ about the current iterate $x_n$ gives

$$
f(x)=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x-x_n)+\frac{1}{2}f''(\xi)(x-x_n)^2,
$$

where $\xi$ lies between $x$ and $x_n$.

## Linear Approximation

If $x$ is near $x_n$, then the quadratic term is small, so we approximate

$$
f(x)\approx f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x-x_n).
$$

To approximate a root, we set $x=x_{n+1}$ and impose

$$
f(x_{n+1})\approx 0.
$$

Thus,

$$
0=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n).
$$

Solving for $x_{n+1}$ gives

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
$$

> [!def] Newton Iteration Formula
> $$
> x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
> $$

---

## Practical Interpretation

Each Newton step consists of:

1. Evaluate $f(x_n)$ and $f'(x_n)$.
2. Compute the correction
   $$
   \Delta x_n=-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
   $$
3. Update
   $$
   x_{n+1}=x_n+\Delta x_n.
   $$

---

# Error Analysis and Convergence

## Setup

Assume $x^*$ is a true root of $f$, so that

$$
f(x^*)=0.
$$

Define the error at step $n$ by

$$
e_n=x^*-x_n.
$$

We now study how $e_{n+1}$ depends on $e_n$.

---

## Deriving the Error Formula

Apply Taylor's theorem to $f(x^*)$ about $x_n$:

$$
f(x^*)=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x^*-x_n)+\frac{1}{2}f''(\xi)(x^*-x_n)^2.
$$

Since $f(x^*)=0$, we get

$$
0=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x^*-x_n)+\frac{1}{2}f''(\xi)(x^*-x_n)^2.
$$

Divide by $f'(x_n)$, assuming $f'(x_n)\neq 0$:

$$
0=\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}+(x^*-x_n)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2.
$$

Rearrange:

$$
x^*=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2.
$$

Using the Newton update formula,

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)},
$$

we obtain

$$
x^*=x_{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2.
$$

Subtract $x_{n+1}$ from both sides:

$$
x^*-x_{n+1}
=
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
e_{n+1}
=
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}e_n^2.
$$

---

## Quadratic Convergence

If $f''$ remains bounded near the root and $f'(x_n)$ stays away from zero, then the factor

$$
-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}
$$

remains bounded. Hence

$$
e_{n+1}\approx M e_n^2
$$

for some constant $M$ near the root.

This means the new error is proportional to the **square** of the old error, which is the hallmark of **quadratic convergence**.

> [!thm] Local Quadratic Convergence of Newton's Method
> Let $f\in C^2$ near a root $r$, and assume
> $$
> f(r)=0
> \quad \text{and} \quad
> f'(r)\neq 0.
> $$
> Then, for any initial guess $x_0$ sufficiently close to $r$, Newton's method converges to $r$ and the convergence is quadratic.
>
> Equivalently, if $e_n=r-x_n$, then
> $$
> \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{e_{n+1}}{e_n^2}
> =
> \frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)}.
> $$
> In particular,
> $$
> |e_{n+1}|
> \approx
> \left|\frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)}\right|\,|e_n|^2
> $$
> for $n$ large.

> [!imp] Important
> The condition $f'(r)\neq 0$ means that $r$ is a **simple root**. If the root is multiple, Newton's method may still converge, but generally not quadratically.

# Solving Systems of Nonlinear Equations

## The Multivariable Problem

Now consider a system of $n$ nonlinear equations in $n$ unknowns:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_1(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)&=0,\\
f_2(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)&=0,\\
&\vdots\\
f_n(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Let

$$
X=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2\\
\vdots\\
x_n
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
F(X)=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_1(X)\\
f_2(X)\\
\vdots\\
f_n(X)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then the problem is to find $X$ such that

$$
F(X)=0.
$$

---

## From 1D Newton to Multivariable Newton

In one dimension, Newton's update can be written as

$$
x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}.
$$

Equivalently, if we define the step

$$
\Delta x = x_{k+1}-x_k,
$$

then Newton's formula becomes

$$
f'(x_k)\Delta x=-f(x_k).
$$

This form generalizes naturally to systems.

### Correspondence Between 1D and $n$D

- Scalar unknown $x$ becomes vector unknown $X$.
- Scalar function $f(x)$ becomes vector function $F(X)$.
- Scalar derivative $f'(x)$ becomes the **Jacobian matrix**.
- Scalar correction $\Delta x$ becomes vector correction $\Delta X$.

---

## The Jacobian Matrix

> [!def] Jacobian Matrix
> The Jacobian of $F$ at $X$ is the matrix of first partial derivatives:
> $$
> DF(X)=
> \begin{bmatrix}
> \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \dots & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\
> \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \dots & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n}\\
> \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
> \dfrac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_2} & \dots & \dfrac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_n}
> \end{bmatrix}.
> $$

The Jacobian is the multivariable analogue of the derivative. It provides the best linear approximation to $F$ near the current iterate.

---

## Newton's Method for Systems

Using the linearization of $F$ near $X_k$,

$$
F(X_k+\Delta X)\approx F(X_k)+DF(X_k)\Delta X,
$$

we want the next iterate to satisfy

$$
F(X_{k+1})\approx 0.
$$

So we choose $\Delta X$ so that

$$
F(X_k)+DF(X_k)\Delta X=0.
$$

Thus we solve the linear system

$$
DF(X_k)\Delta X=-F(X_k),
$$

and then update by

$$
X_{k+1}=X_k+\Delta X.
$$

> [!def] Newton's Method for Systems
> Given an initial guess $X_0$, repeat:
>
> 1. Solve
>    $$
>    DF(X_k)\Delta X=-F(X_k)
>    $$
>    for $\Delta X$.
> 2. Update
>    $$
>    X_{k+1}=X_k+\Delta X.
>    $$

---

## Why We Do Not Explicitly Invert the Jacobian

Although one can write

$$
X_{k+1}=X_k-[DF(X_k)]^{-1}F(X_k),
$$

this is usually **not** how Newton's method is implemented in practice.

> [!imp] Numerical Linear Algebra Principle
> Do **not** explicitly compute the matrix inverse unless there is a special reason to do so.

### Reasons

#### 1. Computational Cost

Computing a matrix inverse is expensive, especially for large systems.

#### 2. Numerical Stability

Explicit inversion can amplify roundoff errors, particularly if the Jacobian is close to singular.

#### 3. Better Alternative

It is more efficient and more stable to solve the linear system

$$
DF(X_k)\Delta X=-F(X_k)
$$

directly using methods such as:

- Gaussian elimination
- LU decomposition
- QR methods
- iterative linear solvers for large sparse problems

---

# Variants and Practical Remarks

## Inexact Newton Method

> [!cor] Inexact Newton Iteration
> Instead of solving
> $$
> DF(X_k)\Delta X=-F(X_k)
> $$
> exactly, one solves it only approximately at each step.

This is often useful for large-scale problems where exact linear solves are too expensive.

## Frozen Jacobian Method

> [!cor] Frozen Jacobian
> Instead of recomputing the Jacobian at every step, one reuses a previously computed Jacobian for several iterations.

This reduces cost per iteration, though it may also reduce convergence speed.

---

# Summary

## Single Equation

For a scalar equation $f(x)=0$, Newton's method is

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
$$

If started sufficiently near a simple root, it converges **quadratically**.

## System of Equations

For a system $F(X)=0$, Newton's method becomes:

$$
DF(X_k)\Delta X=-F(X_k),
\qquad
X_{k+1}=X_k+\Delta X.
$$

The Jacobian plays the role of the derivative, and each step requires solving a linear system.

## Key Idea

In both cases, Newton's method works by replacing a nonlinear problem with its **local linear approximation** and solving that simpler problem repeatedly.
