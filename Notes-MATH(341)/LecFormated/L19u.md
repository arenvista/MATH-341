# Newton's Method for Nonlinear Systems

## Linearization Idea

Suppose we want to solve
$$F(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}, \qquad F:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n.$$

At a current iterate $\vec{x}_k$, linearize $F$:
$$F(\vec{x}_k+\Delta \vec{x}) \approx F(\vec{x}_k) + DF(\vec{x}_k)\,\Delta \vec{x}.$$

To force the linear approximation to vanish, set
$$F(\vec{x}_k) + DF(\vec{x}_k)\,\Delta \vec{x} = \vec{0},$$
which gives the **Newton step**
$$DF(\vec{x}_k)\,\Delta \vec{x} = -F(\vec{x}_k).$$

Then update by
$$\vec{x}_{k+1} = \vec{x}_k + \Delta \vec{x}.$$

> [!def] Newton's Method for Systems
> Given an initial guess $\vec{x}_0$, repeat:
>
> 1. **Solve the linear system**
>    $$DF(\vec{x}_k)\,\Delta \vec{x}_k = -F(\vec{x}_k).$$
>
> 2. **Update**
>    $$\vec{x}_{k+1} = \vec{x}_k + \Delta \vec{x}_k.$$
>
> Both $F(\vec{x}_k)$ and the Jacobian $DF(\vec{x}_k)$ must be recomputed at each iteration.

---

## Local Convergence Theorem

> [!thm] Newton's Method — Local Convergence
> Let $F \in C^1(D)$ on a bounded, closed, convex set $D \subset \mathbb{R}^n$.
>
> Assume $\vec{x}^* \in D$ satisfies
> $$F(\vec{x}^*) = \vec{0}$$
> and
> $$\det(DF(\vec{x}^*)) \neq 0.$$
>
> Then there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that if
> $$\|\vec{x}_0-\vec{x}^*\| < \varepsilon,$$
> the Newton iterates
> $$\vec{x}_{n+1} = \vec{x}_n - [DF(\vec{x}_n)]^{-1}F(\vec{x}_n)$$
> are well-defined and satisfy
> $$\lim_{n\to\infty} \vec{x}_n = \vec{x}^*.$$

### Interpretation

- Newton's method is a **local method**.
- If the initial guess is sufficiently close to a solution with **invertible Jacobian**, convergence is guaranteed.
- If the Jacobian at the solution is singular, the usual convergence theorem does **not** apply.

# Worked Example: A Nonlinear $2\times 2$ System

## Problem Setup

Apply Newton's method to
$$\begin{cases}
x^2 + 4x - 2y - 1 = 0, \\
x^2 + 5y - 4 = 0.
\end{cases}$$

Let
$$F(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_1(x,y)\\
f_2(x,y)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x^2 + 4x - 2y - 1\\
x^2 + 5y - 4
\end{bmatrix}.$$

> [!imp] Correction
> The second equation must be
> $$x^2+5y-4=0,$$
> not $x^2+5y+5=0$.
>
> This correction is consistent with the stated Newton iterate
> $$\Delta \vec{x}=\begin{bmatrix}0.65\\0.8\end{bmatrix}$$
> and with the chosen rectangle $D=[0,2]\times[0,0.8]$.

---

## Step 1: Compute the Jacobian

$$DF(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y}\\[6pt]
\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2x+4 & -2\\
2x & 5
\end{bmatrix}.$$

---

## Step 2: Check Invertibility on a Region

Take
$$D=[0,2]\times[0,0.8].$$

Then
$$\det(DF(x,y)) = (2x+4)(5)-(-2)(2x)=10x+20+4x=14x+20.$$

Since
$$14x+20>0 \qquad \text{for all } (x,y)\in D,$$
the Jacobian is invertible everywhere on $D$.

---

## Step 3: Newton Iteration

### Initial Guess

$$\vec{x}_0=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.$$

### First Iteration

Compute
$$DF(\vec{x}_0)=
\begin{bmatrix}
4 & -2\\
0 & 5
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
F(\vec{x}_0)=
\begin{bmatrix}
-1\\
-4
\end{bmatrix}.$$

Solve
$$DF(\vec{x}_0)\,\Delta \vec{x}_0 = -F(\vec{x}_0)=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.$$

That is,
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
4 & -2\\
0 & 5
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta x\\
\Delta y
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
4
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

From the second equation,
$$5\Delta y=4 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Delta y=0.8.$$

From the first equation,
$$4\Delta x-2(0.8)=1
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
4\Delta x=2.6
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\Delta x=0.65.$$

Hence
$$\Delta \vec{x}_0=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.65\\
0.8
\end{bmatrix}.$$

Update:
$$\vec{x}_1=\vec{x}_0+\Delta \vec{x}_0=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.65\\
0.8
\end{bmatrix}.$$

---

### Second Iteration

Evaluate at $\vec{x}_1=(0.65,0.8)$:
$$F(\vec{x}_1)=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.4225\\
0.4225
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
DF(\vec{x}_1)=
\begin{bmatrix}
5.3 & -2\\
1.3 & 5
\end{bmatrix}.$$

Solve
$$DF(\vec{x}_1)\,\Delta \vec{x}_1 = -F(\vec{x}_1).$$

This gives approximately
$$\Delta \vec{x}_1 \approx
\begin{bmatrix}
-0.1016\\
-0.0581
\end{bmatrix}.$$

So
$$\vec{x}_2=\vec{x}_1+\Delta \vec{x}_1 \approx
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5484\\
0.7419
\end{bmatrix}.$$

Thus the iterates are moving toward the solution.

---

## Exact Solution for Comparison

From the second equation,
$$y=\frac{4-x^2}{5}.$$

Substitute into the first:
$$x^2+4x-2\left(\frac{4-x^2}{5}\right)-1=0.$$

Multiply by $5$:
$$5x^2+20x-8+2x^2-5=0,$$
so
$$7x^2+20x-13=0.$$

Hence
$$x=\frac{-20\pm\sqrt{764}}{14}.$$

The root in $D$ is
$$x^*=\frac{-20+\sqrt{764}}{14}\approx 0.5455,$$
and then
$$y^*=\frac{4-(x^*)^2}{5}\approx 0.7405.$$

So Newton's method is converging to
$$\vec{x}^* \approx
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5455\\
0.7405
\end{bmatrix}.$$

# Example: When the Jacobian Is Singular

## System

Consider
$$\begin{cases}
xy-1=0,\\
x^2+y^2-2=0.
\end{cases}$$

Equivalently,
$$F(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
xy-1\\
x^2+y^2-2
\end{bmatrix}.$$

Its Jacobian is
$$DF(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
y & x\\
2x & 2y
\end{bmatrix}.$$

---

## A Singular Root

At
$$\vec{x}^*=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{bmatrix},$$
we have
$$F(1,1)=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},$$
so $(1,1)$ is a solution.

But
$$DF(1,1)=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1\\
2 & 2
\end{bmatrix},$$
and therefore
$$\det(DF(1,1))=0.$$

> [!imp] Why This Matters
> Since the Jacobian is singular at the solution, the Newton convergence theorem does **not** apply.
>
> - If we start **exactly** at $(1,1)$, we are already at a root, so no iteration is needed.
> - However, **near** this root, the Newton step may be undefined or unstable because it requires inverting $DF(x,y)$.
>
> So the correct conclusion is:
>
> **Newton's method is not guaranteed to behave well near a solution where the Jacobian is singular.**

---

## Geometric Picture

The system
$$xy=1, \qquad x^2+y^2=2$$
describes the intersections of a hyperbola and a circle.

> [!tip] Visualization
> $$\begin{align}
> 0 &= xy - 1 & \implies & \quad y = \frac{1}{x}, \\
> 0 &= x^2 + y^2 - 2 & \implies & \quad x^2 + y^2 = 2.
> \end{align}$$

# Vector Calculus Review

## Scalar-Valued Functions

> [!def] Scalar-Valued Function
> A scalar-valued function has the form
> $$f(x_1,\dots,x_n):\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}.$$

### Gradient

Using the **row-vector convention** in these notes,
$$\nabla f =
\begin{bmatrix}
f_{x_1} & \dots & f_{x_n}
\end{bmatrix}.$$

---

## Vector-Valued Functions

> [!def] Vector-Valued Function
> A vector-valued function has the form
> $$\vec{F}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=
> \begin{bmatrix}
> f_1(x_1,\dots,x_n)\\
> \vdots\\
> f_m(x_1,\dots,x_n)
> \end{bmatrix},
> \qquad
> \vec{F}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m.$$

### Jacobian Matrix

The Jacobian stacks the gradients of the component functions as rows:
$$D\vec{F}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\nabla f_1\\
\vdots\\
\nabla f_m
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
\dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}
\end{bmatrix}.$$

---

## Product Rules

### Dot Product Rule

If $\vec{u},\vec{v}:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, then
$$\nabla(\vec{u}^T\vec{v})
=
\vec{v}^T D\vec{u} + \vec{u}^T D\vec{v}.$$

This is the vector version of the usual product rule.

---

### Matrix–Vector Product Rule

If $A=A(\vec{x})$ is a matrix-valued function and $\vec{v}=\vec{v}(\vec{x})$ is a vector-valued function, then
$$D(A\vec{v}) = A\,D\vec{v} + \sum_{i=1}^{m} v_i\,D\vec{a}_i,$$
where $\vec{a}_i$ is the $i$-th column of $A$.

> [!imp] Special Case: Constant Matrix
> If $A$ is constant, then the second term vanishes and
> $$D(A\vec{v}) = A\,D\vec{v}.$$

# Nonlinear Least Squares and Gauss–Newton

## Review: Linear Least Squares

For an overdetermined linear system
$$A\vec{x}\approx \vec{b},$$
we seek $\bar{\vec{x}}$ minimizing
$$\|\vec{b}-A\vec{x}\|_2.$$

This can be done using:

- normal equations,
- QR factorization,
- SVD.

---

## Nonlinear Least Squares Setup

Suppose the residual functions are
$$r_1(x_1,\dots,x_n),\dots,r_m(x_1,\dots,x_n),$$
and define the residual vector
$$\vec{r}(\vec{x})=
\begin{bmatrix}
r_1(\vec{x})\\
\vdots\\
r_m(\vec{x})
\end{bmatrix}.$$

We minimize the error functional
$$E(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^m r_i(\vec{x})^2
=\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}(\vec{x})^T\vec{r}(\vec{x}).$$

---

## First-Order Optimality Condition

To minimize $E$, we set its gradient equal to zero.

Using the dot product rule,
$$\nabla E(\vec{x})
=
\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}^T\vec{r}\right)
=
\frac{1}{2}\left(\vec{r}^T D\vec{r}+\vec{r}^T D\vec{r}\right)
=
\vec{r}^T D\vec{r}.$$

Thus the row-vector optimality condition is
$$\vec{r}^T D\vec{r}=\vec{0}.$$

Taking transpose gives the equivalent column-vector form
$$D\vec{r}(\vec{x})^T \vec{r}(\vec{x})=\vec{0}.$$

If we write
$$J(\vec{x}) := D\vec{r}(\vec{x}),$$
then the nonlinear normal equations become
$$J(\vec{x})^T \vec{r}(\vec{x})=\vec{0}.$$

---

## Newton's Method Applied to the Normal Equations

Define
$$F(\vec{x}) := J(\vec{x})^T \vec{r}(\vec{x}).$$

To apply Newton's method, we need the derivative of $F$.

A standard calculation gives
$$DF(\vec{x})
=
J(\vec{x})^T J(\vec{x})
+
\sum_{i=1}^m r_i(\vec{x})\,\nabla^2 r_i(\vec{x}).$$

So the exact Newton step $\vec{p}_k$ satisfies
$$\left(J_k^T J_k + \sum_{i=1}^m r_i(\vec{x}_k)\,\nabla^2 r_i(\vec{x}_k)\right)\vec{p}_k
=
-\,J_k^T \vec{r}(\vec{x}_k),$$
where
$$J_k := J(\vec{x}_k).$$

---

## Gauss–Newton Approximation

If the residuals are already small near the minimizer, then the term
$$\sum_{i=1}^m r_i(\vec{x})\,\nabla^2 r_i(\vec{x})$$
is often negligible.

This leads to the **Gauss–Newton approximation**
$$DF(\vec{x}) \approx J(\vec{x})^T J(\vec{x}).$$

Therefore, the Gauss–Newton step $\vec{p}_k$ solves
$$J_k^T J_k\,\vec{p}_k = -J_k^T \vec{r}(\vec{x}_k),$$
and the iterate is updated by
$$\vec{x}_{k+1} = \vec{x}_k + \vec{p}_k.$$

> [!def] Gauss–Newton Method
> For nonlinear least squares:
>
> 1. Form the residual vector $\vec{r}(\vec{x}_k)$.
> 2. Form the Jacobian $J_k=D\vec{r}(\vec{x}_k)$.
> 3. Solve
>    $$J_k^T J_k\,\vec{p}_k = -J_k^T \vec{r}(\vec{x}_k).$$
> 4. Update
>    $$\vec{x}_{k+1}=\vec{x}_k+\vec{p}_k.$$

---

## Analogy with Linear Least Squares

For a linear residual
$$\vec{r}(\vec{x}) = A\vec{x}-\vec{b},$$
the Jacobian is constant:
$$J(\vec{x}) = A.$$

Then Gauss–Newton becomes
$$A^T A\,\vec{p}_k = -A^T(A\vec{x}_k-\vec{b}),$$
which is exactly the normal-equations framework from linear least squares.

So Gauss–Newton is the natural nonlinear analogue of solving
$$A^T A\,\vec{x}=A^T \vec{b}.$$

# Summary

## Newton for Systems
Solve
$$DF(\vec{x}_k)\,\Delta \vec{x}_k=-F(\vec{x}_k),$$
then update
$$\vec{x}_{k+1}=\vec{x}_k+\Delta \vec{x}_k.$$

## Convergence
Local convergence is guaranteed near a root $\vec{x}^*$ if

- $F$ is sufficiently smooth, and
- $DF(\vec{x}^*)$ is invertible.

## Failure Mode
If the Jacobian is singular at the root, the standard Newton theorem does not apply.

## Gauss–Newton
For minimizing
$$E(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\vec{r}(\vec{x})\|_2^2,$$
solve approximately
$$J_k^T J_k\,\vec{p}_k=-J_k^T\vec{r}(\vec{x}_k),$$
then update
$$\vec{x}_{k+1}=\vec{x}_k+\vec{p}_k.$$
