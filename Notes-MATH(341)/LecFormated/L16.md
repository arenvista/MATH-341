# Power Method vs. Inverse Iteration

## Overview

These iterative methods target different parts of the spectrum of a matrix $A$:

| Method | Main Goal | Basic Operation | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power Method | Largest eigenvalue in magnitude | Multiply by $A$ | Dominant eigenpair |
| Block Power Iteration | Several largest eigenvalues | Multiply by $A$ on a block of vectors | Dominant invariant subspace |
| Inverse Iteration | Smallest eigenvalue in magnitude, or eigenvalue near a shift | Solve a linear system | Interior or smallest eigenpairs |
| Rayleigh Quotient Iteration | Rapid refinement of one eigenpair | Solve shifted systems with adaptive shift | High-accuracy local convergence |

---

# The Power Method

## Problem Setup

> [!def] Eigenpair Problem
> Given a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, an eigenpair $(\lambda, v)$ satisfies
> $$
> Av = \lambda v, \qquad v \neq 0.
> $$
> In the basic power method, we seek the eigenpair associated with the eigenvalue of largest magnitude.

For lecture purposes, this method is especially clean when $A$ is **symmetric** (and in particular when $A$ is SPD).

---

## Basic Idea

Repeated multiplication by $A$ amplifies the component of a vector in the direction of the dominant eigenvector.

If $A$ has eigenvalues
$$
|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \cdots \ge |\lambda_n|
$$
with corresponding eigenvectors $q_1,\dots,q_n$, and if
$$
v^{(0)} = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j q_j, \qquad c_1 \neq 0,
$$
then
$$
A^k v^{(0)} = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j \lambda_j^k q_j
= \lambda_1^k \left(c_1 q_1 + \sum_{j=2}^n c_j \left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}\right)^k q_j\right).
$$
After normalization, the terms involving $\left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}\right)^k$ decay, so the iterate aligns with $q_1$.

---

## Algorithm

> [!def] Power Method Algorithm
> Choose $v^{(0)}$ such that $\|v^{(0)}\|_2 = 1$.  
> For $k = 0,1,2,\dots$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> w^{(k+1)} &= A v^{(k)}, \\
> v^{(k+1)} &= \frac{w^{(k+1)}}{\|w^{(k+1)}\|_2}, \\
> \lambda^{(k+1)} &= (v^{(k+1)})^T A v^{(k+1)}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$

The scalar $\lambda^{(k+1)}$ is the **Rayleigh quotient estimate** of the eigenvalue.

---

## Convergence

> [!thm] Convergence of the Power Method
> Suppose $A$ is diagonalizable and has a unique dominant eigenvalue:
> $$
> |\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \cdots \ge |\lambda_n|.
> $$
> If the initial vector has a nonzero component in the $q_1$ direction, then the normalized iterates satisfy
> $$
> v^{(k)} \to \pm q_1
> $$
> and the eigenvalue estimates satisfy
> $$
> \lambda^{(k)} \to \lambda_1.
> $$

### Rate of Convergence

The asymptotic convergence rate is governed by
$$
\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|.
$$
So convergence is slow when the dominant eigenvalue is not well separated from the rest of the spectrum.

> [!imp] Key Limitation
> The power method only finds the eigenvalue of largest magnitude, and it can converge very slowly if
> $$
> |\lambda_2| \approx |\lambda_1|.
> $$

---

## Practical Residual Check

A standard way to test convergence is to compute the residual
$$
r^{(k)} = A v^{(k)} - \lambda^{(k)} v^{(k)}.
$$
If $\|r^{(k)}\|_2$ is small, then $(\lambda^{(k)}, v^{(k)})$ is a good approximate eigenpair.

---

# Block Power Iteration

## Motivation

The single-vector power method finds only one dominant eigenvector. If we want the top $m$ eigenvectors, restarting with deflation is possible but often inefficient.

Block power iteration extends the power method by evolving an entire subspace at once.

---

## Objective

Given a symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, find an orthonormal matrix
$$
V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, \qquad V^T V = I_m,
$$
whose columns approximate the invariant subspace associated with the $m$ largest eigenvalues of $A$.

Usually, $m \ll n$.

---

## Main Idea

Instead of a single vector, start with a block of vectors:
$$
V^{(0)} = [v_1^{(0)} \ \cdots \ v_m^{(0)}].
$$
At each step:

1. Multiply by $A$:
   $$
   \widetilde V^{(k)} = A V^{(k)}.
   $$
2. Re-orthonormalize the columns using QR factorization:
   $$
   \widetilde V^{(k)} = Q^{(k+1)} R^{(k+1)}.
   $$
3. Set
   $$
   V^{(k+1)} = Q^{(k+1)}.
   $$

Without orthonormalization, all columns tend to collapse toward the dominant eigenvector.

---

## Algorithm

> [!def] Block Power Iteration / Subspace Iteration
> Choose $V^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ with orthonormal columns:
> $$
> V^{(0)T}V^{(0)} = I_m.
> $$
> For $k = 0,1,2,\dots$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \widetilde V^{(k)} &= A V^{(k)}, \\
> \widetilde V^{(k)} &= Q^{(k+1)} R^{(k+1)}, \\
> V^{(k+1)} &= Q^{(k+1)}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> After convergence, form the projected matrix
> $$
> B = V^T A V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}.
> $$
> The eigenvalues of $B$ are the **Ritz values**, which approximate the leading eigenvalues of $A$.

---

## Important Clarification About $R$

In the QR step,
$$
A V^{(k)} = Q^{(k+1)} R^{(k+1)},
$$
the matrix $R^{(k+1)}$ is upper triangular, but its diagonal entries are **not** generally the eigenvalues of $A$.

> [!imp] Correct Eigenvalue Extraction
> In block power iteration, the preferred eigenvalue approximations come from the small projected matrix
> $$
> B^{(k)} = V^{(k)T} A V^{(k)},
> $$
> not directly from the diagonal of $R^{(k)}$.

---

## Rayleigh--Ritz Refinement

Once the subspace is accurate, a better approximation to the individual eigenvectors/eigenvalues is obtained by diagonalizing the small matrix:
$$
B = V^T A V = W \Theta W^T.
$$
Then
$$
U = V W
$$
has columns approximating the dominant eigenvectors of $A$, and $\Theta$ contains the corresponding eigenvalue approximations.

---

## Convergence Check

A common stopping criterion is the block residual
$$
\|A V - V(V^T A V)\|_F.
$$
If this is small, then the column space of $V$ is close to an invariant subspace of $A$.

---

## Pseudocode

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{Choose } V^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, \quad V^{(0)T}V^{(0)} = I_m. \\
& \text{for } k = 0,1,2,\dots: \\
& \qquad \widetilde V^{(k)} = A V^{(k)} \\
& \qquad [V^{(k+1)}, R^{(k+1)}] = \mathrm{QR}(\widetilde V^{(k)}) \\
& \qquad B^{(k+1)} = V^{(k+1)T} A V^{(k+1)} \\
& \qquad \text{if } \|A V^{(k+1)} - V^{(k+1)} B^{(k+1)}\|_F \text{ is small, stop.}
\end{aligned}
$$

---

## Remarks

- Also called **subspace iteration** or **simultaneous iteration**.
- Best suited for symmetric or Hermitian matrices.
- Useful when only a few dominant eigenpairs are needed.
- Can be accelerated by shifts, deflation, or Krylov-subspace methods.

---

# Inverse Iteration

## Basic Observation

If
$$
Av = \lambda v,
$$
then, provided $A$ is invertible,
$$
A^{-1} v = \frac{1}{\lambda} v.
$$
So the eigenvalues of $A^{-1}$ are $\frac{1}{\lambda_1},\dots,\frac{1}{\lambda_n}$.

This means the largest eigenvalue in magnitude of $A^{-1}$ corresponds to the smallest eigenvalue in magnitude of $A$.

---

## Shifted Inverse Iteration

More generally, if
$$
Av = \lambda v,
$$
then
$$
(A-\sigma I)^{-1} v = \frac{1}{\lambda-\sigma} v.
$$
Thus, the eigenvalue $\lambda$ closest to the shift $\sigma$ becomes dominant for the inverse of $(A-\sigma I)$.

> [!def] Shifted Inverse Iteration
> Given a shift $\sigma$, choose $v^{(0)}$ with $\|v^{(0)}\|_2 = 1$.  
> For $k=0,1,2,\dots$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (A-\sigma I) w^{(k+1)} &= v^{(k)}, \\
> v^{(k+1)} &= \frac{w^{(k+1)}}{\|w^{(k+1)}\|_2}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$

---

## Interpretation

- If $\sigma = 0$, this is the basic inverse iteration.
- If $\sigma$ is close to some eigenvalue $\lambda_j$, then the method converges to the corresponding eigenvector $q_j$.
- The closer $\sigma$ is to $\lambda_j$, the faster the convergence.

> [!thm] Convergence of Shifted Inverse Iteration
> Assume $A$ is diagonalizable and $\sigma$ is closer to $\lambda_j$ than to any other eigenvalue:
> $$
> |\lambda_j - \sigma| < |\lambda_i - \sigma| \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.
> $$
> Then, provided the initial vector has a nonzero component in the $q_j$ direction, the iterates converge to the eigenvector associated with $\lambda_j$.

### Asymptotic Rate

The convergence factor is roughly
$$
\left|\frac{\lambda_j-\sigma}{\lambda_\ell-\sigma}\right|,
$$
where $\lambda_\ell$ is the second-closest eigenvalue to $\sigma$.

---

## Practical Note

Inverse iteration does **not** form $(A-\sigma I)^{-1}$ explicitly.  
Instead, each step solves the linear system
$$
(A-\sigma I) w = v.
$$

If the same shift $\sigma$ is reused many times, it is efficient to factor
$$
A-\sigma I = LU
$$
(or a symmetric factorization when appropriate) once and reuse it.

---

# Rayleigh Quotient Iteration

## Motivation

Inverse iteration becomes especially powerful if the shift is updated adaptively using the current Rayleigh quotient.

This yields **Rayleigh quotient iteration (RQI)**, one of the fastest local methods for symmetric eigenvalue problems.

---

## Rayleigh Quotient

> [!def] Rayleigh Quotient
> For a nonzero vector $v$, the Rayleigh quotient of $A$ is
> $$
> \rho(v) = \frac{v^T A v}{v^T v}.
> $$
> If $\|v\|_2 = 1$, this simplifies to
> $$
> \rho(v) = v^T A v.
> $$

For symmetric matrices, the Rayleigh quotient is a very good eigenvalue estimate when $v$ is close to an eigenvector.

---

## Algorithm

> [!def] Rayleigh Quotient Iteration
> Choose $v^{(0)}$ with $\|v^{(0)}\|_2 = 1$, and set
> $$
> \mu^{(0)} = (v^{(0)})^T A v^{(0)}.
> $$
> For $k=0,1,2,\dots$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (A-\mu^{(k)}I) w^{(k+1)} &= v^{(k)}, \\
> v^{(k+1)} &= \frac{w^{(k+1)}}{\|w^{(k+1)}\|_2}, \\
> \mu^{(k+1)} &= (v^{(k+1)})^T A v^{(k+1)}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$

---

## Why It Works

Each step is inverse iteration with shift
$$
\sigma = \mu^{(k)} = \rho(v^{(k)}),
$$
and the shift becomes increasingly accurate as the vector improves.

So the method repeatedly tunes itself toward a nearby eigenvalue.

---

## Convergence

> [!thm] Local Convergence of RQI
> For a symmetric matrix $A$, if the initial vector $v^{(0)}$ is sufficiently close to an eigenvector $q_j$, then Rayleigh quotient iteration converges to $(\lambda_j, q_j)$.
> Moreover, the convergence is **cubic**:
> $$
> \|v^{(k+1)} \mp q_j\| = O\!\left(\|v^{(k)} \mp q_j\|^3\right),
> $$
> and similarly the eigenvalue error decreases cubically:
> $$
> |\mu^{(k+1)}-\lambda_j| = O\!\left(|\mu^{(k)}-\lambda_j|^3\right)
> $$
> locally.

> [!imp] Important Correction
> The cubic convergence statement must compare the error at step $k+1$ to the error at step $k$.  
> The correct form is
> $$
> \text{new error} = O((\text{old error})^3),
> $$
> not an expression involving the same iterate on both sides.

---

## Remarks

- RQI is typically much faster than fixed-shift inverse iteration.
- It is especially effective for symmetric/Hermitian matrices.
- It is a **local** method: good initialization matters.
- The system
  $$
  (A-\mu^{(k)}I)w = v^{(k)}
  $$
  can become ill-conditioned near convergence, but this is part of why the method rapidly homes in on the eigenvector.

---

# From Iterative Methods to Dense Eigenvalue Algorithms

## Motivation

If we want only a few extreme eigenpairs, methods like power iteration or inverse iteration may be appropriate.

If we want **most or all** eigenvalues of a dense matrix, the standard approach is different: we use **Schur factorization** computed via the **QR algorithm**.

---

# Schur Factorization

## Definition

> [!def] Schur Factorization
> For any complex square matrix $A$, there exists a unitary matrix $U$ such that
> $$
> A = U T U^*,
> $$
> where $T$ is upper triangular.

Because $A$ and $T$ are similar, they have the same eigenvalues. Since $T$ is triangular, the eigenvalues appear on its diagonal.

---

## Real Schur Form

For a real matrix $A$, one usually computes the **real Schur form**
$$
A = Q T Q^T,
$$
where

- $Q$ is orthogonal,
- $T$ is **quasi-upper-triangular**,
- the diagonal blocks of $T$ are either:
  - $1 \times 1$ blocks for real eigenvalues, or
  - $2 \times 2$ blocks for complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs.

> [!imp] Practical Limitation
> For a real matrix, one cannot in general reduce all the way to a real diagonal matrix if complex eigenvalues are present.  
> That is why the real Schur form has $2\times 2$ blocks.

---

# QR Iteration for Eigenvalues

## Core QR Step

The QR algorithm produces a sequence of similar matrices.

Starting with $A_0 = A$, perform:
$$
A_k = Q_k R_k,
$$
where $Q_k$ is orthogonal (or unitary) and $R_k$ is upper triangular. Then set
$$
A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k.
$$
Since
$$
A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k = Q_k^* A_k Q_k,
$$
the matrices $A_k$ and $A_{k+1}$ are similar, so they have the same eigenvalues.

As $k$ increases, $A_k$ tends toward Schur form.

---

## Two-Phase Computational Workflow

### Phase 1: Reduce to Hessenberg Form

For a general dense matrix, first reduce $A$ to **upper Hessenberg form**:
$$
A = QHQ^*,
$$
where $H$ has zeros below the first subdiagonal:
$$
H =
\begin{bmatrix}
x & x & x & x & x \\
x & x & x & x & x \\
0 & x & x & x & x \\
0 & 0 & x & x & x \\
0 & 0 & 0 & x & x
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This preserves eigenvalues and makes each QR step much cheaper.

> [!def] Hessenberg Matrix
> A matrix is upper Hessenberg if
> $$
> h_{ij}=0 \quad \text{whenever } i > j+1.
> $$

### Phase 2: QR Iteration on the Hessenberg Matrix

Apply QR iteration repeatedly to $H$:
$$
H_0 = H, \qquad
H_k = Q_k R_k, \qquad
H_{k+1} = R_k Q_k.
$$
The iterates converge to Schur form:
- upper triangular in the complex case,
- quasi-upper-triangular in the real case.

---

## Why Hessenberg Reduction Matters

QR factorization of a general dense matrix is expensive.  
If the matrix is already Hessenberg, each QR step is much more efficient while preserving the Hessenberg structure.

So the overall dense eigenvalue workflow is:

1. Reduce $A$ to Hessenberg form.
2. Run shifted QR iteration until convergence to Schur form.
3. Read off the eigenvalues from the resulting triangular or block-triangular matrix.

---

## Symmetric Special Case

If $A$ is symmetric, the reduction is even better:

- first reduce $A$ to **tridiagonal** form,
- then apply a symmetric QR algorithm.

This is cheaper and more stable than the general nonsymmetric case.

---

# Summary of Methods

## Extreme Eigenvalues vs. Full Spectrum

### If you want one dominant eigenpair:
Use the **power method**.

### If you want several dominant eigenpairs:
Use **block power iteration / subspace iteration**.

### If you want an eigenvalue near a chosen target $\sigma$:
Use **shifted inverse iteration**.

### If you want very fast local convergence to a single eigenpair:
Use **Rayleigh quotient iteration**.

### If you want most or all eigenvalues of a dense matrix:
Use the **Hessenberg reduction + QR iteration + Schur form** pipeline.

---

## Final Takeaways

> [!imp] Key Ideas
> - Power iteration amplifies dominant modes by repeated multiplication by $A$.
> - Inverse iteration amplifies modes near a chosen shift by solving linear systems.
> - Rayleigh quotient iteration updates the shift adaptively and converges cubically for symmetric matrices.
> - Modern dense eigensolvers do **not** repeatedly use the power method; instead they reduce to Hessenberg form and apply QR iteration to reach Schur form.
