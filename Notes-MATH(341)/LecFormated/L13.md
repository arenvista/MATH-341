# Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

## Definition

> [!def] Singular Value Decomposition
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$. Its **singular value decomposition** is
> $$
> A = U \Sigma V^T,
> $$
> where:
> - $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ is orthogonal, so $U^T U = I_m$.
> - $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is orthogonal, so $V^T V = I_n$.
> - $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is rectangular-diagonal:
> $$
> \Sigma =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> \sigma_1 &&& 0 \\
> & \sigma_2 && \\
> && \ddots & \\
> 0 &&& \sigma_p
> \end{bmatrix},
> \qquad p = \min(m,n),
> $$
> with singular values ordered as
> $$
> \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_p \ge 0.
> $$

## Terminology

- The columns of $U$ are the **left singular vectors**.
- The columns of $V$ are the **right singular vectors**.
- The nonzero diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ are the **singular values** of $A$.

## Compact SVD

If $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$, then only the first $r$ singular values are nonzero. In that case, the **compact SVD** is

$$
A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T,
$$

where:
- $U_r \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$ has columns $u_1,\dots,u_r$,
- $V_r \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ has columns $v_1,\dots,v_r$,
- $\Sigma_r = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r)$.

This is often the most useful form in computations.

# SVD as a Sum of Rank-One Matrices

## Outer-Product Expansion

From the compact SVD,

$$
A = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^T,
$$

we can write $A$ as

$$
A = \sum_{j=1}^{r} \sigma_j u_j v_j^T.
$$

Each matrix $u_j v_j^T$ is an outer product, hence has rank $1$ (provided $\sigma_j \ne 0$). Therefore, the SVD expresses $A$ as a sum of rank-one matrices.

## Truncated SVD

For any integer $\nu$ with $0 \le \nu \le r$, define the truncated matrix

$$
A_\nu = \sum_{j=1}^{\nu} \sigma_j u_j v_j^T.
$$

Then:
- $A_\nu$ has rank at most $\nu$,
- $A_\nu$ keeps only the $\nu$ largest singular values and their singular vectors.

This is the standard way to build a low-rank approximation to $A$.

# Singular Values, Rank, and Fundamental Subspaces

## Basic SVD Identities

> [!lem] Action of $A$ and $A^T$ on singular vectors
> If $A = U \Sigma V^T$ and $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$, then for $1 \le j \le r$,
> $$
> A v_j = \sigma_j u_j,
> \qquad
> A^T u_j = \sigma_j v_j.
> $$
> For $j > r$,
> $$
> A v_j = 0,
> \qquad
> A^T u_j = 0.
> $$

> [!pf]
> Since $V^T v_j = e_j$,
> $$
> A v_j = U \Sigma V^T v_j = U \Sigma e_j.
> $$
> If $j \le r$, then $\Sigma e_j = \sigma_j e_j$, so
> $$
> A v_j = \sigma_j u_j.
> $$
> If $j>r$, then the $j$th diagonal entry is $0$, so $A v_j=0$. The proof for $A^T u_j$ is identical.

## Rank from Singular Values

> [!thm] Rank Equals the Number of Nonzero Singular Values
> If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has singular values $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_p$, then
> $$
> \operatorname{rank}(A) = \#\{j : \sigma_j \ne 0\}.
> $$

This is immediate from the fact that $\Sigma$ has exactly that many nonzero diagonal entries.

## Fundamental Subspaces from the SVD

> [!thm] Fundamental Subspaces
> Let $\operatorname{rank}(A)=r$. Then:
> $$
> \operatorname{Range}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{u_1,\dots,u_r\},
> $$
> $$
> \operatorname{Range}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_r\},
> $$
> $$
> \operatorname{Null}(A) = \operatorname{span}\{v_{r+1},\dots,v_n\},
> $$
> $$
> \operatorname{Null}(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\{u_{r+1},\dots,u_m\}.
> $$
> In particular, the columns of $U$ and $V$ give orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces.

### Why This Is True

- For $j \le r$, we have $A v_j = \sigma_j u_j$, so each $u_j$ lies in $\operatorname{Range}(A)$.
- Since the vectors $u_1,\dots,u_r$ are orthonormal and there are exactly $r$ of them, they form a basis of $\operatorname{Range}(A)$.
- For $j>r$, $A v_j=0$, so those $v_j$ lie in $\operatorname{Null}(A)$.
- The statements for $A^T$ follow similarly.

# Best Low-Rank Approximation

## Eckart--Young Theorem

> [!thm] Best Rank-$\nu$ Approximation
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ have singular values
> $$
> \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0,
> $$
> and let
> $$
> A_\nu = \sum_{j=1}^{\nu} \sigma_j u_j v_j^T
> $$
> for $0 \le \nu \le r$. Then $A_\nu$ is the best approximation to $A$ among all matrices of rank at most $\nu$:
> $$
> \|A - A_\nu\|_2
> =
> \inf_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le \nu}\|A-B\|_2
> =
> \sigma_{\nu+1}.
> $$
> By convention, if $\nu=r$, then $\sigma_{r+1}=0$.
>
> In Frobenius norm,
> $$
> \|A-A_\nu\|_F^2 = \sum_{j=\nu+1}^{r}\sigma_j^2.
> $$

## Interpretation

The truncated SVD keeps the most important rank-one pieces of $A$ first:
- $\sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T$ contributes the largest part,
- $\sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T$ contributes the next largest part,
- and so on.

So $A_\nu$ is the optimal way to compress $A$ to rank $\nu$.

## Rank Stability Under Perturbation

> [!cor] Full-Rank Stability
> Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ have rank $r$, and assume its smallest nonzero singular value is $\sigma_r>0$. If a perturbation $B$ satisfies
> $$
> \|A-B\|_2 < \sigma_r,
> $$
> then
> $$
> \operatorname{rank}(B) \ge r.
> $$
> In particular, if $A$ has full rank, then $B$ also has full rank.

> [!pf]
> If $\operatorname{rank}(B)\le r-1$, then by Eckart--Young,
> $$
> \|A-B\|_2 \ge \sigma_r,
> $$
> which contradicts $\|A-B\|_2 < \sigma_r$. Hence $\operatorname{rank}(B)\ge r$.

# Numerical Rank

## Exact Rank vs Numerical Rank

In exact arithmetic, the rank is the number of nonzero singular values. In floating-point arithmetic, tiny singular values may appear because of rounding errors, so we often use a **numerical rank**.

> [!def] Numerical Rank
> The numerical rank of $A$ is the number of singular values that are significantly larger than the computational tolerance.

A common practical rule is:
$$
\sigma_k > \text{tol},
\qquad
\sigma_{k+1} \le \text{tol},
$$
where $\text{tol}$ is chosen relative to machine precision, often on the order of
$$
\text{tol} \approx \max(m,n)\,\sigma_1\,\varepsilon_{\text{mach}}.
$$

## Useful Fact

The largest singular value is exactly the matrix 2-norm:
$$
\sigma_1 = \|A\|_2.
$$

# SVD vs. Eigenvalue Decomposition (EVD)

## Eigenvalue Decomposition

If a square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, then it can be diagonalized as

$$
A = X \Lambda X^{-1},
$$

where:
- $X$ contains eigenvectors,
- $\Lambda$ contains eigenvalues.

Not every square matrix is diagonalizable, so EVD does **not** always exist in this form.

## Key Differences

| Feature | SVD | EVD |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Applies to** | Every matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ | Square matrices only |
| **Existence** | Always exists | Requires diagonalizability for $A=X\Lambda X^{-1}$ |
| **Bases used** | Two orthogonal bases: $U$ and $V$ | One basis: $X$ |
| **Orthogonality** | Always orthogonal | Orthogonal only in special cases, e.g. symmetric matrices |
| **Values on diagonal** | Nonnegative singular values | Eigenvalues, which may be negative or complex |

## Relationship Between SVD and EVD

> [!thm] Singular Values from $A^T A$
> The nonzero singular values of $A$ are the square roots of the nonzero eigenvalues of $A^T A$ (equivalently, of $A A^T$).

> [!pf]
> Starting from
> $$
> A = U\Sigma V^T,
> $$
> compute:
> $$
> A^T A
> = (U\Sigma V^T)^T(U\Sigma V^T)
> = V\Sigma^T U^T U \Sigma V^T
> = V(\Sigma^T\Sigma)V^T.
> $$
> Since $V$ is orthogonal, this is an eigenvalue decomposition of $A^T A$. The eigenvalues are the diagonal entries of $\Sigma^T\Sigma$, namely
> $$
> \sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2,\dots.
> $$
> Therefore, the singular values are the square roots of those eigenvalues.

## Important Consequence

The matrices $A^T A$ and $A A^T$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues. Thus one may compute singular values by studying either matrix, typically choosing the smaller one in practice.

# Least Squares via the SVD

## Problem Statement

Given
$$
A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n},
\qquad
b \in \mathbb{R}^m,
$$
we want to solve the least-squares problem
$$
\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n} \|b-Ax\|_2.
$$

If there are infinitely many minimizers, we additionally want the one with smallest norm:
$$
\min \|x\|_2.
$$

## Step 1: Insert the SVD

Let
$$
A = U\Sigma V^T,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(A)=r.
$$

Then
$$
\|b-Ax\|_2
=
\|U^T(b-Ax)\|_2
=
\|U^T b - \Sigma(V^T x)\|_2,
$$
because multiplication by an orthogonal matrix preserves the Euclidean norm.

Define
$$
c = U^T b \in \mathbb{R}^m,
\qquad
y = V^T x \in \mathbb{R}^n.
$$

Then the problem becomes
$$
\min_y \|c-\Sigma y\|_2.
$$

## Step 2: Partition According to the Rank

Write
$$
c =
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat c \\
d
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
y =
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat y \\
z
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat\Sigma & 0 \\
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
$$
where
$$
\hat\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r).
$$

Then
$$
c-\Sigma y
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat c \\
d
\end{bmatrix}
-
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat\Sigma \hat y \\
0
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\hat c - \hat\Sigma \hat y \\
d
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\|c-\Sigma y\|_2^2
=
\sum_{i=1}^r |\hat c_i-\sigma_i \hat y_i|^2
+
\sum_{i=r+1}^{m} |d_i|^2.
$$

## Step 3: Minimize the Error

The second sum is independent of $y$, so it cannot be changed. To minimize the first sum, choose
$$
\hat y_i = \frac{\hat c_i}{\sigma_i},
\qquad i=1,\dots,r.
$$

The remaining components $z$ do not affect the residual at all, because they multiply the zero part of $\Sigma$.

## Step 4: Enforce Minimum Norm

Since $V$ is orthogonal,
$$
\|x\|_2 = \|y\|_2.
$$

Thus among all least-squares solutions, the one with minimum norm is obtained by setting the free variables to zero:
$$
z=0.
$$

So the minimum-norm least-squares solution is determined by
$$
y_i =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{c_i}{\sigma_i}, & 1 \le i \le r,\\[6pt]
0, & r < i \le n.
\end{cases}
$$
and then
$$
x = V y.
$$

## Algorithm Summary

1. Compute the SVD:
   $$
   A = U\Sigma V^T.
   $$
2. Compute
   $$
   c = U^T b.
   $$
3. For $i=1,\dots,r$, set
   $$
   y_i = \frac{c_i}{\sigma_i}.
   $$
4. For $i=r+1,\dots,n$, set
   $$
   y_i = 0.
   $$
5. Recover the solution:
   $$
   x = Vy.
   $$

# The Moore--Penrose Pseudoinverse

## Definition

> [!def] Moore--Penrose Pseudoinverse
> If
> $$
> A = U\Sigma V^T
> $$
> has rank $r$, then its pseudoinverse is
> $$
> A^\dagger = V \Sigma^\dagger U^T,
> $$
> where $\Sigma^\dagger \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ is formed by replacing each nonzero singular value $\sigma_i$ by $1/\sigma_i$:
> $$
> \Sigma^\dagger =
> \begin{bmatrix}
> \hat\Sigma^{-1} & 0 \\
> 0 & 0
> \end{bmatrix}.
> $$

In summation form,
$$
A^\dagger = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \frac{1}{\sigma_i} v_i u_i^T.
$$

## Connection to Least Squares

The pseudoinverse packages the SVD least-squares algorithm into one matrix:
$$
x = A^\dagger b.
$$

> [!thm] Minimum-Norm Least-Squares Solution
> For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the unique vector $x$ that:
> 1. minimizes $\|b-Ax\|_2$, and
> 2. among all such minimizers has the smallest norm $\|x\|_2$,
>
> is
> $$
> x = A^\dagger b.
> $$

> [!pf]
> From the SVD derivation, the least-squares minimizer in rotated coordinates is
> $$
> y = \Sigma^\dagger c,
> \qquad c = U^T b.
> $$
> Since $x = Vy$, we obtain
> $$
> x = V(\Sigma^\dagger c)
> = V\Sigma^\dagger U^T b
> = A^\dagger b.
> $$

## Final Interpretation

- If $A$ is square and invertible, then
  $$
  A^\dagger = A^{-1}.
  $$
- If $Ax=b$ is inconsistent, $A^\dagger b$ gives the best least-squares fit.
- If $Ax=b$ has infinitely many solutions, $A^\dagger b$ picks the one of smallest Euclidean norm.

# Key Takeaways

> [!imp] Main Facts to Remember
> - Every matrix has an SVD:
>   $$
>   A = U\Sigma V^T.
>   $$
> - The singular values determine the rank:
>   $$
>   \operatorname{rank}(A) = \#\{\sigma_i \ne 0\}.
>   $$
> - The singular vectors give orthonormal bases for the four fundamental subspaces.
> - The truncated SVD
>   $$
>   A_\nu = \sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\sigma_j u_j v_j^T
>   $$
>   is the best rank-$\nu$ approximation to $A$.
> - The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^T A$.
> - The minimum-norm least-squares solution is
>   $$
>   x = A^\dagger b.
>   $$
