# Power Method vs. Inverse Iteration
## Power Method

The Power Method is an iterative technique used to approximate the dominant eigenvalue (the one with the largest absolute value) and its corresponding eigenvector of a matrix, especially for symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices.

### Problem Statement
Given an SPD matrix $A$, we seek an eigenpair $(\lambda, v)$ such that:

$$
A v = \lambda v
$$

### Initialization
Choose an initial guess $v^{(0)}$ with unit 2-norm:

$$
\| v^{(0)} \|_2 = 1
$$

### Algorithm
 [!note] **Power Method Algorithm**
$$
 \begin{gathered}
 \text{for } i = 1, 2, \ldots \\
 \quad v^{(i)} = A v^{(i-1)} \\
 \quad v^{(i)} = \frac{v^{(i)}}{\|v^{(i)}\|_2} \quad \text{(normalize)} \\
 \quad \lambda^{(i)} = (v^{(i)})^T A v^{(i)} \quad \text{(Rayleigh quotient estimate)}
 \end{gathered}
$$ 

- **Convergence:** $v^{(i)}$ converges to the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue in magnitude.
- The algorithm is simple but can converge slowly, especially if the dominant eigenvalue is not well-separated from others.



## Block Power Iteration (Power Iteration with Multiple Vectors)

Block power iteration is an efficient algorithm for computing the largest $m$ eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors of a large matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ — especially when only a few dominant eigenpairs are required.

---

### Objective

Given a symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, find a matrix $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ such that
- $V^T V = I_m$
- The columns $(v_1, v_2, ..., v_m)$ of $V$ approximate $A$'s $m$ dominant eigenvectors
- $m \ll n$

---

### Standard Single-Vector Power Iteration (Review)
Repeatedly applies $v_{k+1} = \frac{A v_k}{\|A v_k\|}$ to converge $v_k$ to the dominant eigenvector. To find more than one eigenpair, the process must be restarted with deflation — which is inefficient for multiple eigenvalues.

---

### Block Power Iteration: Key Ideas

- Start with several randomly chosen, orthonormal vectors (columns of $V$)
- At every step, apply $A$ to the _whole block_ (matrix multiplication):
    - $\widetilde{V} = A V$
- Each repeated multiplication emphasizes the dominant subspace (span of largest $m$ eigenvectors)
- **But:** Each $A V$ degrades orthonormality
- **Solution:** Orthonormalize via QR factorization after every matrix multiply

---

### Block Power Iteration Algorithm

#### **Overview**
Block Power Iteration is an extension of the standard power iteration method, designed to approximate the top $m$ eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. This algorithm finds an orthonormal basis for the invariant subspace associated with the $m$ largest eigenvalues.

---

#### **Algorithm Steps**

1. **Initialization**
    - Select an initial matrix $V^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ with orthonormal columns: $V^{(0)T} V^{(0)} = I_m$.
    - *(Tip: Obtain this by sampling a random matrix and applying QR factorization: $V^{(0)} = \mathrm{qr}(\mathrm{randn}(n, m))$)*

2. **Iteration**  
   Repeat for $i = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ until convergence:
    
    1. **Matrix-Multiply:**
        - $\widetilde{V}^{(i)} = A V^{(i)}$
    2. **Orthogonalize (QR Factorization):**
        - $\widetilde{V}^{(i)} = Q^{(i+1)} R^{(i+1)}$
          
        - Set $V^{(i+1)} = Q^{(i+1)}$
    3. **Convergence Check (optional):**
        - Monitor the difference: $\| A V^{(i+1)} - V^{(i+1)} \Lambda^{(i+1)} \|$
        - Here, $\Lambda^{(i+1)} = (V^{(i+1)T} A V^{(i+1)})$ (Rayleigh quotient, see below)

    **Note:** The QR stage in each iteration ensures the column orthogonality of $V^{(i)}$ and numerical stability.

    $$
    A Q_i = \underbrace{Q_{i+1}}_{\substack{\text{Approximate} \\ \text{Eigenvectors }}} \underbrace{R_{i+1}}_{\substack{\text{Upper triangular~} \\ \text{ Diagonal} \approx \text{Eigenvalues}}}
    $$

3. **Eigenvalue Approximation (Rayleigh Quotients):**
    - After convergence, compute the eigenvalue estimates:
      $$
      \Lambda \approx V^T A V
      $$
    - The matrix $\Lambda$ is $m \times m$ (ideally diagonal), with each diagonal entry approximating a dominant eigenvalue of $A$.

---

#### **Summary Table**

| Symbol           | Description                                   |
|------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| $A$              | Input symmetric matrix ($n\times n$)          |
| $m$              | Number of leading eigenvalues/eigenvectors    |
| $V^{(i)}$        | Current $n\times m$ orthonormal basis         |
| $Q^{(i+1)}$      | Orthonormal $n\times m$ factor from QR        |
| $R^{(i+1)}$      | Upper-triangular $m\times m$ factor from QR   |
| $\Lambda$        | Approximate eigenvalues ($m\times m$)         |


---

### Pseudocode (LaTeX)

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{Initialize: choose } V^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}, \text{ with } V^{(0)T}V^{(0)} = I_m \\
& \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, ... : \\
& \hspace{1em} \widetilde{V}^{(k)} = A V^{(k)} \\
& \hspace{1em} [V^{(k+1)}, R^{(k+1)}] = \text{QR}(\widetilde{V}^{(k)}) \\
& \hspace{1em} \text{Compute: } \Lambda^{(k+1)} = V^{(k+1)T} A V^{(k+1)} \\
& \text{Converged? If yes, return } (V^{(k+1)}, \Lambda^{(k+1)})
\end{aligned}
$$

---

### Notes
- **Restoring Orthonormality:** Orthonormalizing after each step (via QR) is essential — otherwise, columns of $V$ collapse onto the dominant eigenvector
- **Connection to Subspace Iteration:** This is also called _subspace iteration_ or _normalized simultaneous iteration_
- **Practical Convergence:** Convergence is typically checked by $\|A V - V \Lambda\|_F$ becoming small (Frobenius norm)
- **Rayleigh Quotient:** $\Lambda = V^T A V$ gives the eigenvalue approximations corresponding to columns of $V$

#### Notes
- Suited for symmetric or positive-definite matrices.
- For faster convergence, can use deflation or shift techniques.


### Applications
- **Power Method**: Good for dominant eigenvalues.
- **Inverse Iteration**: Good for smallest eigenvalues or gaining eigenpairs near a selected shift.


## Inverse Iteration

Inverse Iteration, sometimes called the Inverse Power Method, is used to find the eigenvalue closest to a chosen shift $\sigma$ and its corresponding eigenvector.

### Key Observation

If $Av = \lambda v$, then
$$ A^{-1} v = \frac{1}{\lambda} v $$
So, applying the inverse of $A$ amplifies the component of $v$ corresponding to the eigenvalue with the smallest absolute value.

### Inverse Iteration Algorithm *(summary)*

- Instead of multiplying by $A$, solve $A x = v^{(i-1)}$ each step.
- You can improve the method by shifting: solve $(A - \sigma I) x = v^{(i-1)}$.
- Normalizing as in the power method.

# Rayleigh Quotient Iteration

Rayleigh quotient iteration combines *inverse iteration with shift* and *Rayleigh quotient calculation* for rapid convergence to an eigenpair of a matrix.

## Overview
This algorithm finds an eigenvalue $\lambda$ and its corresponding eigenvector \( \vec{v} \) of a square matrix \( A \) by repeatedly estimating the eigenvalue using the Rayleigh quotient and then solving a shifted linear system.

### Rayleigh Quotient
Given a nonzero vector $v$, the Rayleigh quotient of matrix \( A \) is:
$$
\lambda = \frac{v^T A v}{v^T v}
$$

## Algorithm Steps
1. **Initialization**: Choose an initial vector $v^{(0)}$ with \( \|v^{(0)}\|_2 = 1 \).
2. **Initial Eigenvalue Estimate**: Compute the starting eigenvalue estimate using the Rayleigh quotient:
   $$
   \lambda^{(0)} = (v^{(0)})^T A v^{(0)}
   $$
3. **Iterative Process** (repeat until convergence):
   1. **Solve the Shifted System**:
      $$
      (A - \lambda^{(i-1)} I) v^{(i+1)} = v^{(i-1)}
      $$
   2. **Normalize** $v^{(i+1)}$:
      $$
      v^{(i+1)} = \frac{v^{(i+1)}}{\|v^{(i+1)}\|_2}
      $$
   3. **Update the Eigenvalue Estimate**:
      $$
      \lambda^{(i+1)} = (v^{(i+1)})^T A v^{(i+1)}
      $$
4. **Convergence Check**: If $|\lambda^{(i+1)} - \lambda^{(i)}|$ is below a specified tolerance or \( v^{(i+1)} \) stabilizes, stop and return \( (\lambda, v) \).


**Notes:**
- The method converges cubically for symmetric (Hermitian) matrices.
- Initial vector $v^{(0)}$ should not be orthogonal to the eigenvector sought.
- Normally, the method converges to the eigenvalue nearest the initial Rayleigh quotient.

Remark: Convergence is cubic! Thus is 
$$
\underbrace{ \left\| v^{k+1} - (\pm \underset{Eigenvector}{q_j}) \right\|}_{\text{ Norm of Error at Step $k+1$}} = 
\underbrace{ O(\left\| v^{k+1} - (\pm \underset{Eigenvector}{q_j}) \right\| }_{\text{Norm of Error at Step $k$}}
$$ 

# Eigenvalue Computation: Schur & QR Factorizations, Computational Workflow, and Practical Considerations

## Motivation
Suppose we are interested in finding most (if not all) of the eigenvalues of a matrix $A$. What is the state-of-the-art method for computing them efficiently and accurately?

---

## Schur Factorization
The **Schur decomposition** expresses a matrix $A$ as:
$$
A = U T U^*
$$
where:
- $A$ and $T$ are **similar** matrices (i.e., $A$ and $T$ have the same eigenvalues),
- $T$ is **upper triangular** (for complex matrices) or **block upper triangular** (for real matrices with $2 \times 2$ blocks for complex conjugate eigenvalues),
- $U$ is **unitary** ($U^* U = I$).

---

## QR Factorization (Practical Algorithm)
In practice, the eigenvalue problem is often solved using QR-based algorithms. The workflow involves repeated factorizations:
$$
A = Q R Q^T
$$
where:
- $Q$ is **orthogonal** ($Q^T = Q^{-1}$ in the real case),
- $R$ is **almost upper triangular** (may have $2 \times 2$ blocks along the diagonal for complex conjugate pairs).

---

## Overview of the Computation

### Ideal Algorithm (Iterative Similarity Transforms)
Repetitive similarity transformations are applied:
$$
\dots Q_2^* Q_1^* A Q_1 Q_2 \dots \to T
$$
- Each step moves $A$ closer to an upper triangular (Schur) form.

### Two Main Phases
1. **Phase 1: Reduction to Hessenberg Form**
    - Any general (non-Hermitian) matrix $A$ is first reduced to **Hessenberg form** $H$:
    $$
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x & x & x & x & x \\
        x & x & x & x & x \\
        x & x & x & x & x \\
        x & x & x & x & x \\
        x & x & x & x & x \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \xrightarrow{\text{Phase 1, Finish Matrix Steps}}
    \begin{bmatrix}
          s   & x & x & x & x \\
      \textbf{x} & x & x & x & x \\
            &   \textbf{x} & x & x & x \\
            &         &   \textbf{x} & x & x \\
            &         &         &   \textbf{x} & x \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    - **Hessenberg form** has zero entries below the first subdiagonal (all entries below $\textbf{x}$ are zero).

2. **Phase 2: QR Iteration (Hessenberg $\to$ Schur)**
    - Successive QR iterations are performed to drive more sub-diagonal entries to zero:
    $$
    \begin{bmatrix}
        s   & x & x & x & x \\
        x & x & x & x & x \\
          & x & x & x & x \\
          &   & x & x & x \\
          &   &   & x & x \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \xrightarrow{\text{Phase 2, Iteration; Remove $\textbf{x}$}}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x & x & x & x & x \\
          & x & x & x & x \\
          &   & x & x & x \\
          &   &   & x & x \\
          &   &   &   & x \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    $$
    - The end result is **almost** upper triangular (Schur form).

---

## Practical Considerations & Limitations
- **Phase 2 Limitation**: We cannot generally go all the way to completely diagonal form in the real case, because we need to apply $Q$ from both left and right, which can destroy the sparsity (become less efficient).
- Some matrices (especially real matrices with complex eigenvalues) will yield $2 \times 2$ blocks along the diagonal in $R$ or the Schur form.
- The QR algorithm and Schur decomposition are foundational in numerical linear algebra for robust eigenvalue computation.

---

## Summary
**State-of-the-art eigenvalue computation** for dense matrices generally involves:
1. **Reduction to Hessenberg form**
2. **Repeated QR iterations** until convergence to Schur (or quasi-triangular) form.

These steps yield all (or most) eigenvalues of $A$ in a numerically stable and reliable manner.
