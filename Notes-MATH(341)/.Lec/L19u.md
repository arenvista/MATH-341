> [!tip] ### Newton's Method for Systems
> To find the roots of $F(\vec{X}) = \vec{0}$, we use an iterative process. Let $\vec{X}_k$ be the current guess (where $k$ is the iteration number). The algorithm entails two core steps per iteration:
>
> **Step 1: Solve the Linear System**
> Instead of dividing by the derivative, we solve the following system of linear equations for the unknown update vector $\Delta\vec{X}$:
> $$DF(\vec{X}_k)\,\Delta\vec{X} = -F(\vec{X}_k)$$
> *(Note: Both the Jacobian $DF(\vec{X}_k)$ and the function values $F(\vec{X}_k)$ must be re-evaluated at every single iteration.)*
>
> **Step 2: Update the Guess**
> Once $\Delta\vec{X}$ is found, update the current position to get the next approximation:
> $$\vec{X}_{k+1} = \vec{X}_k + \Delta\vec{X}$$

---

# Theorem: Newton's Method — Convergence Criteria

Let $F(\vec{x})$ be continuously differentiable on a bounded, closed, and convex set $D$ (i.e., $F \in C^1(D)$).

- **Convex Set:** For any two points in $D$, the line segment connecting them lies entirely within $D$.

Suppose $\vec{x}^* \in D$ is a solution to $F(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$, and $\det(DF(\vec{x}^*)) \neq 0$ (i.e., the Jacobian is invertible at $\vec{x}^*$).

Then there exists $\epsilon > 0$ such that for any initial $\vec{x}_0$ with $\|\vec{x}_0 - \vec{x}^*\| < \epsilon$, the Newton sequence $\{\vec{x}_n\}$ defined by

$$\vec{x}_{n+1} = \vec{x}_n - [DF(\vec{x}_n)]^{-1} F(\vec{x}_n)$$

converges to $\vec{x}^*$:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \vec{x}_n = \vec{x}^*$$

## Example: Newton's Method Applied to a Nonlinear System

**Problem.** Formulate and apply Newton's method to solve:

$$\begin{array}{ccccccc}
x^2 & + & 4x & - & 2y & - & 1 & = 0 \\
x^2 &   &    & + & 5y & + & 5 & = 0
\end{array}$$

### Step 1: Compute the Jacobian $DF$

$$DF(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y} \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x+4 & -2 \\ 2x & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

### Step 2: Verify Invertibility on $D$

Let $D = [0,2] \times [0, 0.8]$. Compute the determinant:

$$\det[DF(x,y)] = 5(2x+4) - (2x)(-2) = 10x + 20 + 4x = 14x + 20 > 0 \quad \forall\, \vec{x} \in D$$

**Recall:** $f_1, f_2 \in C^1(D)$ means both component functions are continuously differentiable on $D$. Since the determinant is strictly positive on $D$, the Jacobian is invertible throughout, and Newton's method is applicable.

### Step 3: Newton's Method Iterations

#### Initial Guess

$$\vec{x}_0 := \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

#### Iteration 1

Jacobian at $\vec{x}_0$:
$$DF(\vec{x}_0) = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Function value at $\vec{x}_0$:
$$F(\vec{x}_0) = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solve for the Newton step $\Delta\vec{x}$:
$$DF(\vec{x}_0)\,\Delta\vec{x} = -F(\vec{x}_0)$$

This yields:
$$\Delta\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.65 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$$

#### Update

$$\vec{x}_1 = \vec{x}_0 + \Delta\vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.65 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$$

Proceed by computing $DF(\vec{x}_1)$ and $F(\vec{x}_1)$, solving $DF(\vec{x}_1)\,\Delta\vec{x} = -F(\vec{x}_1)$, updating $\vec{x}_2 = \vec{x}_1 + \Delta\vec{x}$, and continuing iteratively.

### Example: A Case Where Newton's Method May Fail

For the system $xy - 1 = 0$ and $x^2 + y^2 - 2 = 0$, the Jacobian is:

$$DF(\vec{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} y & x \\ 2x & 2y \end{bmatrix}$$

Consider $\vec{x}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$f_1(1,1) = 1 \cdot 1 - 1 = 0, \qquad f_2(1,1) = 1^2 + 1^2 - 2 = 0$$

So $\vec{x}_0 = \vec{x}^*$ is already the solution — but we want a numerical verification via Newton's method. Evaluating the Jacobian at $\vec{x}^*$:

$$DF(\vec{x}^*) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \implies \det[DF(\vec{x}^*)] = 0$$

**Therefore, Newton's method may not converge** at this point, since the convergence theorem requires $\det(DF(\vec{x}^*)) \neq 0$.

# Vector Calculus

### Scalar-Valued Function

A scalar-valued function is written as $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$.

### Gradient

The gradient of $f$ is the vector of partial derivatives:

$$\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} f_{x_1}, & \dots, & f_{x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

### Vector-Valued Function

A vector-valued function $\vec{F}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$:

$$\vec{F}(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \begin{bmatrix} f_1(x_1, \dots, x_n) \\ \vdots \\ f_m(x_1, \dots, x_n) \end{bmatrix}$$

### Jacobian (Matrix of Gradients)

The Jacobian of $\vec{F}$ stacks the gradients of each component function as rows:

$$D\vec{F}(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \begin{bmatrix} \nabla f_1 \\ \vdots \\ \nabla f_m \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}$$

### Vector Dot Product Rule

The product rule for the dot product of vector-valued functions $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ is:

$$\nabla(\vec{u}^T \vec{v}) = \vec{v}^T D\vec{u} + \vec{u}^T D\vec{v}$$

where $D\vec{u}$, $D\vec{v}$ are the Jacobian matrices of $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, respectively.

### Matrix–Vector Product Rule

For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ and a differentiable vector-valued function $\vec{v}$, the Jacobian of the product is:

$$D(A\vec{v}) = A\,D\vec{v} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i\, D\vec{a}_i$$

where $\vec{a}_i$ is the $i$-th column of $A$.

# Newton's Least Squares (Gauss–Newton Method)

**Recall:** In the linear case for $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$, we minimize $\|b - A\vec{x}\|_2$ to find $\bar{\vec{x}}$ using one of:

$$\bar{\vec{x}} \begin{cases} \text{Normal equations} \\ \text{QR factorization} \\ \text{SVD} \end{cases}$$

### Nonlinear Least Squares Setup

Consider a nonlinear residual system:

$$\begin{gathered} r_1(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0 \\ \vdots \\ r_m(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0 \end{gathered}$$

Define the error functional:

$$E(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \frac{1}{2}(r_1^2 + \dots + r_m^2) = \frac{1}{2}\vec{r}^T \vec{r}$$

To minimize $E$, we set $\nabla E = \vec{0}$. That is, we seek $\vec{x}$ such that $F(\vec{x}) := \nabla E(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$.

### Deriving the Normal Equations

Expanding using the vector dot product rule:

$$\vec{0} = F(\vec{x}) = \nabla E = \nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\vec{r}(\vec{x})^T \vec{r}(\vec{x})\right) = \frac{1}{2}\nabla(\vec{r}^T\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{2}\left[\vec{r}^T D\vec{r} + \vec{r}^T D\vec{r}\right] = \vec{r}^T D\vec{r}$$

Taking the transpose to get the column-vector form $F^T(\vec{x}) = \vec{0}$:

$$F^T = (\vec{r}^T D\vec{r})^T = (D\vec{r})^T \vec{r} = \vec{0}$$

where $D\vec{r}$ is the Jacobian matrix of $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{r}$ is the residual vector.

### Gauss–Newton Approximation

Differentiating $F^T$ to form the Jacobian required for Newton's method:

$$DF^T = D\!\left((D\vec{r})^T \vec{r}\right) = (D\vec{r})^T D\vec{r} + \sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i\, D\vec{c}_i$$

where $\vec{c}_i$ are the columns of $D\vec{r}$. Setting $A := D\vec{r}$ (the residual Jacobian):

$$DF^T = A^T A + \Sigma \approx A^T A$$

The second term $\Sigma$ is dropped when residuals are small, yielding the **Gauss–Newton approximation** — a direct analogy to the normal equations $A^T A\,\vec{x} = A^T \vec{b}$ from linear least squares.
