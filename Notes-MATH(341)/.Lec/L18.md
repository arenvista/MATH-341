## Intermediate Value Theorem

## Newton's Method: An Overview

**Newton’s Method** is a root-finding technique used to approximate solutions of an equation of the form:
$$f(x)=0$$

The main idea is to replace the nonlinear function $f(x)$ with its linear approximation near a current guess. Since a tangent line is often much easier to work with than the original curve, this provides an efficient way to produce increasingly accurate approximations to a root.

Geometrically, Newton’s Method begins at the point $(x_n, f(x_n))$ on the graph of $f$. The tangent line at that point is drawn, and the x-intercept of this tangent line becomes the next approximation, $x_{n+1}$. Repeating this process often produces a sequence that converges to an actual root.

### Advantages and Limitations

- **Pros:** It can converge very quickly when the initial guess is close to the root and when $f'(x_n) \neq 0$.
- **Cons:** It may fail if the initial guess is poor, if the derivative is zero or very small, or if the function behaves badly near the root.

---

## Deriving Newton's Method via Taylor's Theorem

To understand exactly where Newton's Method comes from, we can look at **Taylor's Theorem**. Taylor's Theorem provides an exact representation of $f(x)$ near a point $x_n$:

$$f(x) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x-x_n) + \frac{1}{2} f''(\xi)(x-x_n)^2$$

_(where $\xi$ is some value between $x$ and $x_n$)_

### The Linear Approximation

If $x$ is close to $x_n$, the squared term containing $f''$ becomes extremely small. We can drop it to create a linear approximation of the function:

$$f(x) \approx f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x-x_n)$$

### The Iteration Step

Suppose we want to find the root where the function equals zero. We set our next guess, $x_{n+1}$, as the point where this linear approximation crosses the x-axis:

$$0 = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)$$

Now, we solve for $x_{n+1}$:

$$-f(x_n) = f'(x_n)(x_{n+1}-x_n)$$
$$\frac{-f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} = x_{n+1}-x_n$$

Rearranging this gives us the standard **Newton iteration formula**:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$

> [!tip] **In Summary:**
>
> - Newton's Method uses the tangent line at the current guess $x_n$ to find a better guess for the root.
> - Each iteration steps from $x_n$ to $x_{n+1}$ by finding where this tangent line crosses the x-axis.
> - This approach comes directly from using Taylor's Theorem to linearize $f(x)$ near $x_n$.

---

## Error Analysis and Convergence

We can also use Taylor's Theorem to prove _how fast_ Newton's Method converges. Let's assume there exists a true root $x^*$ such that $f(x^*)=0$.

Substituting $x^*$ into the full Taylor expansion around our current guess $x_n$, we get:

$$f(x^*) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x^*-x_n) + \frac{1}{2}f''(\xi)(x^*-x_n)^2$$

Since $f(x^*) = 0$, we can set the left side to zero and divide the entire equation by $f'(x_n)$:

$$0 = \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} + (x^*-x_n) + \frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2$$

Now, let's rearrange the equation to isolate the true root $x^*$:

$$x^* = \underbrace{x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}_{x_{n+1}} - \frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2$$

Notice that the bracketed term is exactly our formula for the next iteration, $x_{n+1}$. Substituting this in, we get:

$$x^* = x_{n+1} - \frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}(x^*-x_n)^2$$

### Quadratic Convergence

Let $e_n$ represent the error at step $n$ (so, $e_n = x^* - x_n$) and $e_{n+1}$ represent the error at the next step ($e_{n+1} = x^* - x_{n+1}$). We can rewrite the equation above in terms of the error:

$$e_{n+1} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)} e_n^2$$

Let the fractional term act as a bounding constant $M$ (assuming the second derivative behaves reasonably and the first derivative isn't zero):
$$M = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{f''(\xi)}{f'(x_n)}$$

This yields the relationship for **quadratic convergence**:
$$e_{n+1} \approx M e_n^2$$

This shows that once you are close to the root, the error at the next step is proportional to the _square_ of the current error, which is why Newton's method converges so rapidly!

Theorem:

Let $f \in  C^2$ and $f(r) > 0$. If $f'(r) \neq 0$, then Newton's method is locally and quadratically convergent to $r$

Moreover the error $e_i$ at step $i$ statisfies:

$$
	\lim_{i \to \infty} \frac{e_{i+1}}{e_i^2} = M
$$

Where

$$
	M = \left| \frac{f''(x)}{2f'(x)} \right|
$$


## Solving Systems of Nonlinear Equations

When moving from single-variable calculus to multivariable systems, solving for the roots of nonlinear equations requires adapting our existing tools. Below is a detailed breakdown of how Newton's Method scales to $n$ dimensions, linking the core concepts together.

---

### Defining the Multivariable System

Consider a system of $n$ nonlinear equations with $n$ variables. Let $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]^T$ be our vector of variables. We want to find the roots where all functions simultaneously equal zero:

$$
    \begin{align}
         & f_1(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = 0 \\
         & f_2(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = 0 \\
         & \vdots                        \\
         & f_n(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = 0
    \end{align}
$$

We can represent this compactly in vector notation as seeking the vector $X$ such that $F(X) = 0$, where $F$ is a vector-valued function.

---

### Bridging 1D to n-Dimensions: The Newton-Raphson Analogy

To solve this system, we can adapt the 1D Newton-Raphson method. 

**Recall the 1D case:**
For a single variable, the iterative update formula is:
$$
    x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}
$$

By multiplying both sides by $f'(x_k)$ and rearranging, we can express this as a linear equation to solve for the step size, $\Delta x$:
$$
    f'(x_k) \underbrace{(x_{k+1}-x_k)}_{\Delta x} = -f(x_k)
$$

**The N-Dimensional Analogue:**
In multidimensional space, division by a vector or matrix is undefined. Therefore, the rearranged linear equation form ($f'(x_k) \Delta x = -f(x_k)$) is the foundation for the multivariable approach. 
* The scalar function $f(x)$ becomes the vector field $F(X)$.
* The step size $\Delta x$ becomes a vector $\Delta X$.
* The scalar derivative $f'(x)$ is replaced by the **Jacobian Matrix**.

---

### The Jacobian Matrix ($DF(X)$)

The Jacobian matrix represents the best linear approximation (or first-order partial derivatives) of the vector-valued function near a given point. It is the multivariable equivalent of the derivative $f'(x)$:

$$
    DF(X) =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \dots  & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\
        \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \dots  & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n} \\
        \vdots                            & \vdots                            & \ddots & \vdots                            \\
        \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_2} & \dots  & \frac{\partial f_n}{\partial x_n} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
$$

---

### Newton's Method for Systems

To find the roots of $F(X) = 0$, we use an iterative process. Let $X_k$ be the current guess (where $k$ is the iteration number). The algorithm entails two core steps per iteration:

**Step 1: Solve the Linear System**
Instead of dividing by the derivative, we solve the following system of linear equations for the unknown update vector $\Delta X$:
$$
    DF(X_k) \Delta X = -F(X_k)
$$
*(Note: Both the Jacobian $DF(X_k)$ and the function values $F(X_k)$ must be re-evaluated at every single iteration).*

**Step 2: Update the Guess**
Once $\Delta X$ is found, update the current position to get the next approximation:
$$
    X_{k+1} = X_k + \Delta X
$$

---

### Computational Critique: Why We Don't Invert the Jacobian

Mathematically, you could write the multivariable Newton update step exactly like the 1D formula by using the inverse of the Jacobian matrix:
$$
    X_{k+1} = X_k - [DF(X_k)]^{-1} F(X_k)
$$

While algebraically correct, **this is considered a poor implementation in computer science and numerical analysis** for several reasons:

* **Computational Expense:** Calculating the exact inverse of an $n \times n$ matrix is an $O(n^3)$ operation. Doing this at every single iteration for large systems is massively inefficient.
* **Numerical Instability:** Matrix inversion is highly susceptible to floating-point errors. If the Jacobian is "ill-conditioned" (close to singular), calculating the inverse will amplify rounding errors, ruining your approximation.
* **The Better Alternative:** It is significantly faster and more stable to solve the linear system $DF(X_k) \Delta X = -F(X_k)$ for $\Delta X$ directly using methods like **Gaussian Elimination** or **LU Decomposition**. You do not need to know what $[DF(X_k)]^{-1}$ is; you only need to know what $\Delta X$ is.


> [!cor] Remark: There are Two Ways of Doing This (What is this Newton's)
**Inexact Newton's Iteration**
The linear system is solved only approximately
**"Frozen Jacobian"**
DE is not updated in every step
