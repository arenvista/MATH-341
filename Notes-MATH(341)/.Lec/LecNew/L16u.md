## **Computing Eigenvalues**

### **The Traditional Textbook Route**
In introductory linear algebra, eigenvalues are found analytically using the following steps:
1.  **Formulate the Characteristic Equation:** Set up the equation $\det(A-\lambda I) = 0$.
2.  **Solve for Roots:** Find the roots of the resulting $n$-degree polynomial to get the eigenvalues ($\lambda$).

**Why this fails in practice:**
* **Theoretical Limitations:** Due to the Abel-Ruffini theorem, there is no general closed-form algebraic formula for finding the roots of polynomials of degree $n \geq 5$. 
* **Numerical Instability:** Root-finding for high-degree polynomials is notoriously ill-conditioned. Even microscopic floating-point rounding errors in the polynomial's coefficients can drastically alter the computed roots (eigenvalues).

**Conclusion:** Because the textbook route is practically impossible for large matrices, we must rely on numerical, iterative algorithms to approximate eigenvalues.

---

### **The Rayleigh Quotient**

Before diving into specific iterative algorithms (like Power Iteration), we need a reliable way to evaluate our approximations. This is where the Rayleigh Quotient comes in.

**Assumptions & Definitions**
* **The Matrix:** Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$. We assume $A$ is **semi-simple** (meaning it is diagonalizable, and its algebraic multiplicities match its geometric multiplicities).
* **The Quotient:** For any non-zero vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the Rayleigh Quotient $r(x)$ is a scalar defined as:
    $$r(x) = \frac{x^T A x}{x^T x}$$

**The Core Property:** If $x$ is an exact eigenvector of $A$, then evaluating the Rayleigh Quotient yields exactly the corresponding eigenvalue $\lambda$:
$$Ax = \lambda x \implies r(x) = \frac{x^T (\lambda x)}{x^T x} = \lambda \frac{x^T x}{x^T x} = \lambda$$

---

### **The Least Squares Perspective**

The true power of the Rayleigh Quotient is how it behaves when $x$ is *not* a perfect eigenvector, but rather an approximation. 

**The Question:** Given an arbitrary vector $x$, what scalar $\alpha$ acts "most like" an eigenvalue for $x$? Specifically, how do we choose $\alpha$ to minimize the residual error length $\| Ax - \alpha x \|_2$?

**The Answer:** We can frame this as a linear Least Squares problem. We are trying to find the scalar $\alpha$ that best satisfies the overdetermined system:
$$x \alpha \approx Ax$$

To find the optimal $\alpha$, we apply the normal equations by multiplying both sides by $x^T$ (projecting the problem into a space where the error is minimized):
$$x^T (x \alpha) = x^T (Ax)$$
$$(x^T x) \alpha = x^T A x$$

Solving for our scalar $\alpha$ gives us the exact formula for the Rayleigh Quotient:
$$\alpha = \frac{x^T A x}{x^T x} = r(x)$$

**Takeaway:** The Rayleigh Quotient $r(x)$ is mathematically the **best possible approximation** of an eigenvalue for any given vector $x$. In computational algorithms, as our vector $x$ iteratively gets closer to a true eigenvector, $r(x)$ rapidly converges to the true eigenvalue.

## **The Power Iteration Algorithm**

Power Iteration is an iterative numerical method used to find the **dominant eigenvalue** (the one with the largest absolute value) and its corresponding eigenvector. 

### **The Algorithm**

To begin, choose a random initial vector $v^{(0)}$ such that its length is normalized: $\|v^{(0)}\|_2 = 1$. Then, for each iteration $k = 1, 2, \dots$, perform the following steps:

1.  **Multiply:** Compute the new direction vector by applying the matrix $A$ to the previous vector.
    $$w = A v^{(k-1)}$$
2.  **Normalize:** Scale the resulting vector so it maintains a length of 1. This prevents the values from blowing up to infinity or shrinking to zero.
    $$v^{(k)} = \frac{w}{\|w\|}$$
3.  **Check Convergence (Optional but Recommended):** Compute the Rayleigh Quotient at this step to approximate the eigenvalue. As $v^{(k)}$ converges to the true eigenvector, $\lambda^{(k)}$ converges to the dominant eigenvalue.
    $$\lambda^{(k)} = (v^{(k)})^T A v^{(k)}$$

Repeat these steps until the difference between $\lambda^{(k)}$ and $\lambda^{(k-1)}$ falls below a desired tolerance.

---

### **Why Does it Work? (Proof of Convergence)**

To understand why repeatedly multiplying by $A$ isolates the dominant eigenvector, we need to look at what happens under the hood algebraically. 

**The Assumptions:**
1. Let $A$ be a diagonalizable matrix with eigenvectors $\{q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\}$ that form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
2. Let the eigenvalues be ordered by their absolute magnitudes, such that there is a strictly dominant eigenvalue: 
   $$|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \geq \dots \geq |\lambda_n|$$

**The Setup:**
Because the eigenvectors form a basis, our random initial vector $v^{(0)}$ can be written as a linear combination of these eigenvectors:
$$v^{(0)} = a_1 q_1 + a_2 q_2 + \dots + a_n q_n$$
*(Note: For the algorithm to work, we must assume $a_1 \neq 0$. In practice, choosing a random initial vector almost guarantees this).*

**The Iteration:**
After $k$ iterations, the vector $v^{(k)}$ is essentially $v^{(0)}$ multiplied by $A^k$, scaled by some normalization constant $c_k$:
$$v^{(k)} = c_k A^k v^{(0)}$$

If we apply $A^k$ to our basis expansion, each eigenvector $q_i$ gets scaled by its corresponding eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ raised to the $k$-th power:
$$v^{(k)} = c_k \left( a_1 \lambda_1^k q_1 + a_2 \lambda_2^k q_2 + \dots + a_n \lambda_n^k q_n \right)$$

**Isolating the Dominant Term:**
To see what happens as $k \to \infty$, factor out the dominant eigenvalue term, $\lambda_1^k$:
$$v^{(k)} = c_k \lambda_1^k \left( a_1 q_1 + a_2 \left( \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} \right)^k q_2 + \dots + a_n \left( \frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_1} \right)^k q_n \right)$$

**The Limit:**
Because we assumed that $\lambda_1$ is strictly the largest eigenvalue in magnitude, all the fractions $\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}$ are strictly less than $1$. 

Therefore, as $k \to \infty$, any fraction less than $1$ raised to the $k$-th power shrinks to $0$:
$$\lim_{k \to \infty} \left( \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1} \right)^k = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \geq 2$$

**The Conclusion:**
All terms inside the parenthesis eventually vanish except for the first one. 
$$v^{(k)} \approx c_k \lambda_1^k (a_1 q_1)$$

The resulting vector $v^{(k)}$ aligns perfectly with $q_1$, the eigenvector corresponding to the dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. The other "directions" ($q_2$ through $q_n$) are entirely overpowered and filtered out by the iteration.

## **Inverse Iteration and Eigenvalue Shifts**

**Recall:** There is a symbiotic relationship between eigenvalues and eigenvectors. If you have an eigenvalue, you can solve for its eigenvector using the null space. Likewise, if you have an approximated eigenvector, you can find its corresponding eigenvalue using the **Rayleigh Quotient**.

### **The Problem with Standard Power Iteration**
Standard Power Iteration has two major limitations:
1.  **Slow Convergence:** The rate of convergence depends on the ratio of the first and second dominant eigenvalues: $\left| \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} \right|$. If $\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} \approx 1$, the algorithm converges painfully slowly.
2.  **Limited Scope:** It only ever finds the *largest* (dominant) eigenvalue. What if we want to find the smallest eigenvalue, or an eigenvalue near a specific value?

### **The Solution: Eigenvalue Shifts and Inversion**
We can mathematically "amplify" the gap between eigenvalues or make *any* targeted eigenvalue the dominant one by applying a mathematical **shift** ($\mu$).

Starting with the standard eigenvalue equation, we subtract $\mu x$ from both sides:
$$Ax = \lambda x$$
$$Ax - \mu x = \lambda x - \mu x$$
$$(A - \mu I)x = (\lambda - \mu)x$$

This shows that shifting a matrix by $\mu I$ shifts its eigenvalues by $\mu$, but *keeps the exact same eigenvectors*. 

To turn our targeted eigenvalue into the largest one, we invert the matrix. Assuming $\mu$ is chosen to be close to an eigenvalue $\lambda_j$ (but $\mu \neq \lambda_j$ so that the matrix remains invertible), we get:
$$(A - \mu I)^{-1} x = \frac{1}{\lambda - \mu} x$$

**The Amplification Effect:** Because we chose $\mu \approx \lambda_j$, the term $(\lambda_j - \mu)$ becomes a very small fraction (e.g., $0.001$). Therefore, its reciprocal $\frac{1}{\lambda_j - \mu}$ grows massively (e.g., $1000$). This makes $\frac{1}{\lambda_j - \mu}$ the strictly dominant eigenvalue of the inverted matrix, forcing rapid convergence.

---

### **The Inverse Iteration Algorithm**

Here is the formalized iterative loop. 
*Define an initial random normalized vector $v^{(0)}$ such that $\|v^{(0)}\|_2 = 1$. Choose a shift value $\mu$.*

For each iteration $k = 1, 2, \dots$:

1.  **Solve the Linear System:** Instead of multiplying, we solve for $w$.
    $$(A - \mu I)w = v^{(k-1)}$$ 
    *(This is mathematically equivalent to $w = (A - \mu I)^{-1} v^{(k-1)}$)*
2.  **Normalize:** Scale the resulting vector to prevent floating-point overflow.
    $$v^{(k)} = \frac{w}{\|w\|_2}$$
3.  **Compute Rayleigh Quotient:** Approximate the eigenvalue of the original matrix $A$.
    $$\lambda^{(k)} = (v^{(k)})^T A v^{(k)}$$

---

### **Why We Don't Compute the Explicit Inverse**
In linear algebra, you should almost **never** compute the explicit inverse matrix $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$ computationally. 

1.  **Computational Cost:** Finding an explicit inverse is extremely expensive—requiring $O(n^3)$ operations.
2.  **Numerical Instability:** Matrix inversion is highly susceptible to rounding errors, which corrupts the data.
3.  **The Better Way:** Instead of finding the inverse matrix, we treat $(A - \mu I)w = v^{(k-1)}$ as a standard system of linear equations ($Ax = b$). In practice, algorithms compute the **LU Decomposition** of $(A - \mu I)$ exactly once before the loop begins. Then, during each iteration, solving for $w$ using forward and backward substitution only takes $O(n^2)$ operations. It is vastly faster and more stable.

---

### **Corollaries: Stability and Convergence**

1.  **Stability in Ill-Conditioned Matrices:** Because we choose $\mu \approx \lambda_j$, the matrix $(A - \mu I)$ is nearly singular (highly ill-conditioned). Paradoxically, Inverse Iteration performs exceptionally well here. The massive numerical errors introduced by the near-singularity actually occur *in the exact direction of the dominant eigenvector we are trying to find*. The normalization step (Step 2) scales down the magnitude of the error, leaving us with a perfectly aligned eigenvector.
2.  **The Convergence Estimate:**
    Let $q_j$ be the exact eigenvector corresponding to our targeted eigenvalue $\lambda_j$, and let $\lambda_i$ be the *next closest* eigenvalue to $\mu$. The error after $k$ iterations shrinks according to:
    $$\| v^{(k)} - [\pm q_j] \| \approx O\left( \left| \frac{\mu - \lambda_j}{\mu - \lambda_i} \right|^k \right)$$
    This shows that convergence is linear, but highly accelerated. The closer $\mu$ is to $\lambda_j$, the smaller the fraction becomes, meaning the error drops to zero incredibly fast.
