# The Gram-Schmidt Algorithm

**Use Case:** The primary application of the Gram-Schmidt process is computing the **QR Factorization** of a matrix, as well as finding orthogonal bases for general subspaces.

**Objective:** Transform a set of linearly independent vectors $B = \{ x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n \}$ into an **orthonormal basis** $U = \{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n \}$.

### Step-by-Step Approach (Induction)

**1. The Base Case ($k=1$):**
We simply take the first vector and normalize it so its length is $1$.
$$u_1 = \frac{x_1}{\|x_1\|}$$

**2. The Inductive Step:**
Suppose we have already successfully created an orthonormal set for the first $k-1$ vectors: $U_{k-1} = \{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_{k-1}\}$. 
We now want to add $u_k$ such that $\text{Span}(U_k) = \text{Span}(B_k)$, and $u_k$ is completely orthogonal to all previous vectors in $U_{k-1}$.

Recall from the Generalized Fourier Expansion that we can project $x_k$ onto the existing orthonormal basis. To find the orthogonal part, we take $x_k$ and *subtract* its projection onto every previously computed $u_i$:

$$v_k = x_k - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \langle u_i | x_k \rangle u_i$$

Finally, we normalize this new orthogonal vector to get $u_k$:

$$u_k = \frac{v_k}{\|v_k\|}$$

### Matrix Notation

Let $U_{k-1}$ be a matrix containing our established orthonormal vectors as columns:
$$U_{k-1} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & \dots & u_{k-1} \end{bmatrix}$$

The projection of $x_k$ onto all these basis vectors can be written as a matrix-vector product:
$$
U_{k-1}^T x_k = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    u_1^T x_k \\
    u_2^T x_k \\
    \vdots \\
    u_{k-1}^T x_k
\end{bmatrix}
$$
*(Note: Each $u_i^T x_k$ is an inner product, resulting in a scalar).*

Multiplying this by $U_{k-1}$ gives us the actual projected vector:
$$U_{k-1} (U_{k-1}^T x_k) = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (u_i^T x_k)u_i$$

Therefore, the orthogonal portion ($v_k$) is original vector minus the projected vector:
$$v_k = x_k - U_{k-1} U_{k-1}^T x_k = (I - U_{k-1} U_{k-1}^T) x_k$$

Normalizing this yields the formal definition of $u_k$ for any $k \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$:
$$u_k = \frac{(I - U_{k-1} U_{k-1}^T) x_k}{\|(I - U_{k-1} U_{k-1}^T) x_k\|}$$

> [!warning] Numerical Instability
> This standard approach is called **Classical Gram-Schmidt (CGS)**. While mathematically perfect, it is highly numerically unstable in computer arithmetic. Floating-point rounding errors cause the later $u_k$ vectors to slowly lose their strict orthogonality.

# QR Factorization

Suppose we have a matrix $A$ with linearly independent columns:
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix}$$

Applying the Gram-Schmidt process to the columns of $A$ yields an orthogonal matrix $Q$:
$$Q = \begin{bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & \dots & q_n \end{bmatrix}$$

From our Gram-Schmidt formula, we know that any original vector $a_k$ can be reconstructed by adding its orthogonal component to its projected components:
$$a_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \langle q_i | a_k \rangle q_i + \|v_k\| q_k$$

We can express this entire system of equations elegantly as a matrix multiplication $A = QR$, where $R$ stores all the projection scalars and norms:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
    a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
    q_1 & q_2 & \dots & q_n
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
    \|v_1\| & \langle q_1 | a_2 \rangle & \langle q_1 | a_3 \rangle & \dots & \langle q_1 | a_n \rangle \\
    0       & \|v_2\|                   & \langle q_2 | a_3 \rangle & \dots & \langle q_2 | a_n \rangle \\
    0       & 0                         & \|v_3\|                   & \dots & \langle q_3 | a_n \rangle \\
    \vdots  & \vdots                    & \vdots                    & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0       & 0                         & 0                         & \dots & \|v_n\|
\end{bmatrix}
$$

* **$Q$** is an $m \times n$ matrix with orthonormal columns.
* **$R$** is an $n \times n$ upper-triangular matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries.

### QR vs. LU Factorization

| Feature | QR Factorization | LU Factorization |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Uniqueness** | Unique (if $A$ has full column rank and diagonals of $R$ are positive). | Unique (if no row swaps are needed and diagonals of $L$ are $1$). |
| **Existence Matrix Shape** | Exists for **rectangular** matrices ($m \times n$) with linearly independent columns. | Only exists for **square** ($n \times n$), non-singular matrices. |
| **Solving Systems** | Used to solve $Ax=b \implies QRx = b$<br>$\implies Rx = Q^Tb$ | Used to solve $Ax=b \implies LUx=b$<br>Solve $Lc=b$, then $Ux=c$ |
| **Substitution Steps** | Requires **1** Back-substitution. | Requires **2** substitutions (Forward then Backward). |
| **Least Squares** | Perfectly adapted to solve overdetermined least squares problems ($\|Ax-b\|_2$). | Cannot be directly used for least squares. |

# Modified Gram-Schmidt (MGS)

To solve the numerical instability of Classical Gram-Schmidt, we use **Modified Gram-Schmidt (MGS)**.

Recall the single-vector orthogonal projector:
$$E_i = I - u_i u_i^T$$

In CGS, we compute the total projection matrix $(I - U_k U_k^T)$ and apply it to $x_k$ all at once. 
Let's see what happens if we apply the individual projectors $E_i$ sequentially instead. Consider the product $E_2 E_1$:

$$
\begin{align*}
    E_2 E_1 &= (I - u_2 u_2^T)(I - u_1 u_1^T) \\
            &= I - u_1 u_1^T - u_2 u_2^T + u_2 u_2^T u_1 u_1^T 
\end{align*}
$$

Because $u_1$ and $u_2$ are orthogonal, their inner product $u_2^T u_1 = 0$. This means the entire cross-term vanishes!
$$u_2 (u_2^T u_1) u_1^T = u_2 (0) u_1^T = 0$$

Therefore:
$$E_2 E_1 = I - u_1 u_1^T - u_2 u_2^T = I - U_2 U_2^T$$

**The Core Insight:**
Mathematically, applying the projections sequentially is *exactly identical* to applying them all at once:
$$E_{k-1} E_{k-2} \dots E_1 = I - U_{k-1} U_{k-1}^T$$

**Why does this matter?** In code, instead of doing $x_k - \text{proj}_{u_1}(x_k) - \text{proj}_{u_2}(x_k) \dots$, MGS updates the vector iteratively:
1. $x_k^{(1)} = E_1 x_k$
2. $x_k^{(2)} = E_2 x_k^{(1)}$
3. ...and so on.

By projecting against the *updated* vector rather than the original vector, floating-point rounding errors are minimized, making MGS vastly more numerically stable.
