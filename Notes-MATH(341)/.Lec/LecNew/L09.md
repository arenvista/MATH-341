# Least Squares Approximation

When we have an overdetermined linear system (more equations than unknowns, meaning $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ where $m > n$), a perfect solution $Ax = b$ rarely exists. Instead, we seek the "best possible" solution $\hat{x}$ that minimizes the error.

## 1. Geometric Perspective

If the columns of $A$ (e.g., $\vec{v_1}, \vec{v_2}$) span a plane, the equation $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ only has an exact solution if $\vec{b}$ lies exactly within that column space. 

When $\vec{b}$ is outside the plane, the best approximation $A\hat{x}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto the column space of $A$. 
* Therefore, the residual error vector $r = b - A\hat{x}$ must be orthogonal to every vector in the column space of $A$.
* Mathematically: $(Ax)^T (b - A\hat{x}) = 0 \implies x^T A^T (b - A\hat{x}) = 0$.

Since this must hold for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we can drop the $x^T$ to arrive at the **Normal Equations**:
$$A^T(b - A\hat{x}) = 0 \implies A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$$

Solving this system gives the least squares solution $\hat{x}$.

## 2. Application: Fitting Data

**Goal:** Minimize the sum of the squared residuals (errors) between the observed data $y_i$ and the model's predictions $\hat{y}_i$.
$$\text{Minimize } \sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

### The General Algorithm
1.  **Gather Data:** Collect your set of data points $\{(x_i, y_i)\}$.
2.  **Choose a Model:** Decide on the functional form to fit the data (e.g., linear $\hat{y} = ax+b$, quadratic $\hat{y} = ax^2+bx+c$).
3.  **Formulate the System:** Substitute the data points into the model to build the matrix $A$ and vector $b$.
4.  **Solve:** Use the Normal Equations ($A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$) to find the optimal coefficients.
5.  **Evaluate:** Calculate the root-mean-square error (RMSE) to assess the fit: $\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \|b - A\hat{x}\|_2^2}$.

## 3. Examples: Fitting a Line and a Parabola

**Given Data Points:** $(-1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 0), (2, -2)$

### Finding the Best Fit Line ($\hat{y} = ax + b$)
Set up the overdetermined system $Ax = b$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
-1 & 1 \\
0 & 1 \\
1 & 1 \\
2 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Form the Normal Equations ($A^TA\hat{x} = A^Tb$):
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} -5 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Solving this yields $a = -0.9$ and $b = 0.2$.
**Result:** $\hat{y} = -0.9x + 0.2$

### Finding the Best Fit Parabola ($\hat{y} = ax^2 + bx + c$)
Set up the matrix $A$ where the columns correspond to $x^2$, $x$, and $1$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & 1 \\
1 & 1 & 1 \\
4 & 2 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Solving the corresponding Normal Equations yields:
**Result:** $\hat{y} = -0.25x^2 - 0.65x + 0.45$

> [!important] Remark on Numerical Stability
> The condition number of $A^TA$ is the square of the condition number of $A$: $\kappa(A^TA) = (\kappa(A))^2$. This means forming $A^TA$ can drastically amplify numerical errors, making the Normal Equations unstable for ill-conditioned matrices.

## 4. Data Linearization for Non-Linear Models

If a model is non-linear but strictly positive, we can often use logarithms to linearize it so we can still use standard least squares.

**1. Exponential Model:** $y = c_1 e^{c_2 t}$
Take the natural log of both sides:
$$\ln(y) = \ln(c_1 e^{c_2 t})$$
$$\ln(y) = \ln(c_1) + c_2 t$$
By substituting $Y = \ln(y)$ and $k = \ln(c_1)$, we get a linear model: $Y = c_2 t + k$.

**2. Power Law Model:** $y = c_1 t^{c_2}$
Take the natural log of both sides:
$$\ln(y) = \ln(c_1 t^{c_2})$$
$$\ln(y) = \ln(c_1) + c_2 \ln(t)$$
By substituting $Y = \ln(y)$, $X = \ln(t)$, and $k = \ln(c_1)$, we get a linear model: $Y = c_2 X + k$.

## 5. Better Alternatives: QR and SVD

Because calculating $A^TA$ squares the condition number, numerical computing libraries prefer using **QR Factorization** or **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** to solve least squares directly from $A$.

### Using QR Factorization
Substitute $A = QR$ (where $Q$ is orthogonal and $R$ is upper triangular) into the least squares objective function:
$$\min \|Ax - b\|_2 = \min \|QRx - b\|_2$$

Because multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $Q^T$ does not change the $L_2$ norm (length) of a vector, we can multiply the inside by $Q^T$:
$$\|Q^T(QRx - b)\|_2 = \|Rx - Q^Tb\|_2 = \|r\|_2$$

Let $d = Q^Tb$. Since $R$ is upper triangular with zeros below the main diagonal block, the system splits into two parts:
$$
\begin{bmatrix} r_{1} \\ r_{2} \end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} R_{1} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} x - 
\begin{bmatrix} d_{1} \\ d_{2} \end{bmatrix}
$$

To minimize this norm, we set the top part to zero: $R_1 x = d_1$. We can easily solve this using backward substitution without ever calculating $A^TA$! The remaining bottom part, $\|d_2\|_2$, is the minimum residual error.
