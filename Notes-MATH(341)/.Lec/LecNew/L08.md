## 1. Elementary Orthogonal Projectors

An elementary orthogonal projector decomposes a vector $x$ into two perpendicular components: one parallel to a given vector $u$, and one perpendicular to it.

### Definition
Given a normalized column vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}$ where $\|u\| = 1$:
* **Projector onto the orthogonal complement of $u$ ($u_\perp$):** $$Q = I - uu^T$$
* **Projector onto $u$:** $$P_u = uu^T$$

> [!important] The Unnormalized Case
> If $u$ is not normalized ($\|u\| \neq 1$), the projector must be scaled by the inner product of $u$ with itself:
> $$Q = I - \frac{uu^T}{u^Tu}$$

### Key Properties
Any vector $x$ can be written as the sum of its orthogonal projections:
$$x = P_u x + Qx$$

* **Orthogonality:** The two components are strictly orthogonal, meaning $\langle P_ux, Qx \rangle = 0$, or $(P_u x) \perp (Q x)$.
* **Length of the projection:** The length (or norm) of the projection of $x$ onto $u$ is the absolute value of their dot product:
    $$\|P_u x\| = |\langle u, x \rangle| = |u^T x|$$

> [!note] Remark: The "L" Matrix (Gauss Transform)
> In your notes, you asked: *"TODO: What is L?"*
> $$L = I - \alpha e_i e_j^T$$
> **Answer:** This is a **Gauss Transform** (or elementary elimination matrix). While $Q$ and $R$ are *orthogonal* matrices used in QR factorization, $L$ is a *lower triangular* matrix used to perform elementary row operations (specifically, subtracting a multiple of row $j$ from row $i$). This is the foundation of **LU Decomposition**, not QR.

## 2. Elementary Reflectors (Householder Reflectors)

While a projector flattens a vector onto a plane, a reflector acts like a mirror, reflecting a vector across a plane (the plane perpendicular to $u$).

### Definition
$$R = I - 2uu^T$$
*(If $u$ is unnormalized: $R = I - 2\frac{uu^T}{u^Tu}$)*

### Mathematical Properties
Householder Reflectors have two vital properties that make them incredibly useful in numerical computing:
* **Orthogonal:** $R^T R = I \implies R^T = R^{-1}$
* **Symmetric:** $R^T = R$

Because they are both symmetric and orthogonal, applying the reflection twice returns the original vector: 
$$R^2 = RR = I$$

### "Sparsifying" (Zeroing) a Vector
Reflectors are famously used to introduce zeros into a vector. Given a vector $x$:
1.  Let $e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \end{bmatrix}^T$ be the standard basis vector.
2.  Choose the reflection vector $u = x \pm \|x\|e_1$.
3.  Applying the reflector $R$ to $x$ mirrors it directly onto the $e_1$ axis, zeroing out all entries except the first:
    $$Rx = \mp \|x\|e_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \mp \|x\| \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

## 3. Plane Rotators (Givens Rotations)

Instead of reflecting across a plane, Givens Rotations rotate a vector by an angle $\theta$ within a specific 2D coordinate plane to introduce zeros one at a time.

### The 2D Rotator
$$P = \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{bmatrix}$$
* $P$ rotates a vector counter-clockwise.
* $P^T$ rotates a vector clockwise.
* $P$ is orthogonal ($P^T P = I$).

### Plane Rotators ($n \times n$ matrix)
To rotate within the $i$-$j$ plane of an $n$-dimensional space, we embed the $2 \times 2$ rotation matrix into the identity matrix at the $i$ and $j$ intersections:

$$P_{i,j} = \begin{bmatrix} 
1 & \dots & 0 & \dots & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots & & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 
0 & \dots & c & \dots & -s & \dots & 0 \\ 
\vdots & & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 
0 & \dots & s & \dots & c & \dots & 0 \\ 
\vdots & & \vdots & & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
0 & \dots & 0 & \dots & 0 & \dots & 1 
\end{bmatrix}$$

### Calculating $c$ and $s$
To zero out the $j$-th component ($x_j$) of a vector $x$ using the $i$-th component ($x_i$):
$$c = \frac{x_i}{\sqrt{x_i^2 + x_j^2}} \quad \text{and} \quad s = \frac{x_j}{\sqrt{x_i^2 + x_j^2}}$$

Applying $P_{i,j}$ results in:
$$P_{i,j}x = \begin{bmatrix} \vdots \\ \sqrt{x_i^2 + x_j^2} & \leftarrow \text{pos } i \\ \vdots \\ 0 & \leftarrow \text{pos } j \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

### Sequential Zeroing
By chaining rotators together, we can zero out an entire vector from the bottom up, accumulating the total length into the first position:
$$P_{1,n} \dots P_{1,3} P_{1,2} x = \begin{bmatrix} \|x\| \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

> [!tip] Remark: Reflectors vs. Rotators
> **Householder Reflectors** zero out multiple sub-diagonal elements at once and are preferred for dense matrices due to computational efficiency. **Givens Rotators** zero out one element at a time and are highly preferred for sparse or banded matrices.

## 4. Householder Reduction (QR Factorization)

Householder reflectors are applied iteratively to a matrix $A_{m \times n}$ to transform it into an upper triangular matrix $T$ (often denoted $R$ in $A=QR$).

Given $A = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \dots & a_n \end{bmatrix}$:

* **Step 1:** Look at the first column $x = a_1$. Construct a reflector vector $u = x \pm \|x\|e_1$. Create the reflector matrix $R_1 = I - 2\frac{uu^T}{u^Tu}$.
* **Step 2:** Apply $R_1$ to $A$. The first column is zeroed out below the first entry (let $t_{1,1} = \pm \|a_1\|$).
    $$R_1 A = \left[ \begin{array}{c|ccc} t_{1,1} & & \vec{t_1}^T & \\ \hline 0 & & & \\ \vdots & & A_2 & \\ 0 & & & \end{array} \right]$$
* **Step 3:** Move to the submatrix $A_2$. Create a new reflector $R_2$ (padded with a $1$ at the top left so it doesn't affect the first row/column) and apply it.
    $$R_2 R_1 A = \left[ \begin{array}{cc|cc} t_{1,1} & * & & \vec{t_1}^T \\ 0 & t_{2,2} & & \vec{t_2}^T \\ \hline 0 & 0 & & \\ \vdots & \vdots & & A_3 \\ 0 & 0 & & \end{array} \right]$$
* **Step 4:** Repeat this process $n-1$ times (or $m-1$ times). Let $P = R_{n-1} \dots R_2 R_1$. You are left with an upper triangular matrix $T$.
    $$PA = T$$
    
Since each $R_k$ is orthogonal and symmetric, $P$ is orthogonal. Therefore $P^{-1} = P^T = Q$, giving us the final QR factorization:
$$A = QT$$
