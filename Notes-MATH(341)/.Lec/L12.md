# Matrix Decompositions: EVD and SVD

## I. Eigenvalue Decomposition (EVD)
The standard Eigenvalue Decomposition applies only to square matrices. Specifically, if a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD), its eigenvectors are orthogonal, and it can be elegantly decomposed as:
$$A = X \Lambda X^T$$
Where $X$ is the orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors, and $\Lambda$ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

## II. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
What if $A$ is a rectangular matrix ($n \times m$), or a square matrix that is not symmetric/diagonalizable? The **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)** generalizes the concept of eigendecomposition to *any* $n \times m$ matrix. 

$$A = U \Sigma V^T$$

> [!important] Motivations for SVD
> 1. **Algebraic:** Every matrix, regardless of its shape or properties, is orthogonally equivalent to a diagonal matrix. 
> 2. **Geometric:** The range of the unit sphere under any $n \times m$ matrix transformation is a hyperellipse. SVD perfectly captures this rotational and scaling transformation.

> **Definition: Orthogonal Equivalence**
> Two matrices $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ are **equivalent** if there exist non-singular (square) matrices $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ such that $A = XBY$. 
> If $X$ and $Y$ are strictly orthogonal matrices, then $A$ and $B$ are **orthogonally equivalent**.

---

## III. Formal Definition of SVD
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ be a non-zero matrix with rank $r$. Then $A$ can be factored as $A = U \Sigma V^T$ (and consequently $A^T = V \Sigma^T U^T$), where:

* **$U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$**: An orthogonal matrix ($U^T U = I$). Its columns are the **left singular vectors** ($u_i$).
* **$V \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$**: An orthogonal matrix ($V^T V = I$). Its columns are the **right singular vectors** ($v_i$).
* **$\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$**: A rectangular "diagonal" matrix containing the strictly positive **singular values** ($\sigma_i$) corresponding to the rank of the matrix.

The matrix $\Sigma$ is structured such that the singular values are sorted in descending order:
$$
\Sigma =
\begin{bmatrix}
    \sigma_1 &          &        &          &   \\
             & \sigma_2 &        &          &   \\
             &          & \ddots &          &   \\
             &          &        & \sigma_r &   \\
             &          &        &          & 0 
\end{bmatrix} 
\quad \text{where } \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r > 0
$$

## IV. Geometric Perspective
SVD beautifully breaks down a linear transformation into three distinct geometric steps:
1.  **$V^T$**: A rotation (or reflection) in the domain space $\mathbb{R}^m$.
2.  **$\Sigma$**: An independent axis scaling that stretches the space.
3.  **$U$**: A rotation (or reflection) into the codomain space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Let $S$ be the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^m$, and let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ (where $m \leq n$). The image $AS$ under the transformation $T(\vec{v}) := A \vec{v}$ forms a hyperellipse. 
* The right singular vectors ($v_i$) are the orthogonal axes of the unit sphere before transformation.
* The singular values ($\sigma_i$) represent the lengths of the principal semiaxes of the resulting hyperellipse.
* The scaled left singular vectors ($\sigma_i u_i$) define the actual position and orientation of those semiaxes in the output space.

$$A v_i = \sigma_i u_i$$

## V. Matrix Action and the Four Fundamental Subspaces
By mapping between the orthonormal basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ and the orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the action of $A$ and $A^T$ strictly defines the sub-spaces:

$$
A \vec{v}_i = 
\begin{cases} 
\sigma_i u_i & i=1, 2, \dots, r \\ 
0 &  i=r+1, \dots, m 
\end{cases}
\quad \quad \quad 
A^T u_i = 
\begin{cases} 
\sigma_i v_i & i=1, 2, \dots, r \\ 
0 &  i=r+1, \dots, n 
\end{cases}
$$ 

Because of this exact mapping, the columns of $U$ and $V$ perfectly form the orthonormal bases for the **Four Fundamental Subspaces** of matrix $A$:

* **$\text{Range}(A)$** (Column Space): $\text{Span}\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$
* **$\text{Null}(A)$** (Null Space): $\text{Span}\{v_{r+1}, \dots, v_m\}$
* **$\text{Range}(A^T)$** (Row Space): $\text{Span}\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$
* **$\text{Null}(A^T)$** (Left Null Space): $\text{Span}\{u_{r+1}, \dots, u_n\}$

**Fundamental Subspace Theorem Recall:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{Range}(A) &= (\text{Null}(A^T))^\perp = \text{Range}(\text{Row}(A))\\
    \text{Range}(A^T) &= (\text{Null}(A))^\perp 
\end{aligned}
$$

## VI. Reduced (Truncated) SVD
Often, $n$ and $m$ are massive, but the rank $r$ is small (or we only care about the most dominant $k$ singular values to save memory/computation).
Instead of calculating the full SVD, we can compute the **Reduced SVD** by only keeping the first $k$ columns of $U$, the top $k \times k$ sub-matrix of $\Sigma$, and the first $k$ rows of $V^T$:
$$A \approx \hat{U} \hat{\Sigma} \hat{V}^T$$
*This is the mathematical foundation for Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and data compression.*

## VII. Applications of SVD

### 1. Matrix Norms
Because $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal, they do not change the "length" or "magnitude" of vectors. All the magnitude information of matrix $A$ is isolated inside $\Sigma$.

**The 2-Norm (Spectral Norm):**
The maximum stretching a matrix can apply to a vector is exactly its largest singular value.
$$\|A\|_2 = \max \left( \frac{\|Ax\|_2}{\|x\|_2} \right) = \sigma_1$$

**The Frobenius Norm:**
The Frobenius norm is the square root of the sum of the absolute squares of its elements. Through SVD, we can prove it is simply the square root of the sum of the squared singular values.
$$
\|A\|_F = \left( \sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2 \right)^{1/2} = \left( \text{trace}(A^TA) \right)^{1/2}
$$
Substitute $A = U\Sigma V^T$:
$$A^TA = (V\Sigma U^T)(U\Sigma V^T) = V\Sigma^2 V^T$$
Since the trace of a matrix is invariant under cyclic permutation ($\text{trace}(XYZ) = \text{trace}(ZXY)$):
$$
\text{trace}(V\Sigma^2 V^T) = \text{trace}(\Sigma^2 V^TV) = \text{trace}(\Sigma^2 I) = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2
$$
Therefore:
$$\|A\|_F = \|\Sigma\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2}$$

### 2. Matrix Condition Number
The condition number $\kappa(A)$ dictates how sensitive a linear system is to numerical errors.
$$\kappa_2(A) = \|A\|_2 \|A^{-1}\|_2$$

Using SVD, if $A = U \Sigma V^T$, then its inverse is:
$$A^{-1} = (U \Sigma V^T)^{-1} = V^{-T} \Sigma^{-1} U^{-1} = V \Sigma^{-1} U^T$$
The singular values of $A^{-1}$ are simply the reciprocals of the singular values of $A$. Since the 2-norm is the maximum singular value:
$$
0 < \sigma_1^{-1} \leq \dots \leq \sigma_{n-1}^{-1} \leq \sigma_n^{-1}  \implies \|A^{-1}\|_2 = \sigma_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sigma_n}
$$

Thus, the condition number is the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value:
$$\kappa_2(A) = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_n}$$

**Why QR is better than Normal Equations for Least Squares:**
When using Normal Equations ($A^TAx = A^Tb$), the condition number squares:
$$\kappa(A^TA) = (\kappa(A))^2$$
This heavily amplifies floating-point errors, validating the necessity of using $QR$ or $SVD$ to solve complex systems without squaring the matrix!
